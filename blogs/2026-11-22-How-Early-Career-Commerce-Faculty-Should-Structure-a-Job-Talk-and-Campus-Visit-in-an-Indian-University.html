How Early-Career Commerce Faculty Should Structure a Job Talk and Campus Visit in an Indian University So the Selection Committee Leaves with a Clear Research and Teaching Signal

In many Indian universities the formal selection-committee interview for a commerce faculty post is now preceded or accompanied by a job talk, a teaching demonstration, or both, plus a day of informal conversations with department members. Candidates who treat the job talk as a conference presentation and the teaching demonstration as a shortened lecture often leave the committee with a list of papers but no clear sense of what the candidate would contribute to the department’s research pipeline or classroom practice. This briefing shows how to structure the talk, the demonstration, and the surrounding campus interactions so that the committee walks away with a bounded, memorable signal on both dimensions.

Why the Job Talk and Campus Visit Now Carry More Weight Than the CV in Indian Commerce Appointments

Commerce departments in Indian universities operate under simultaneous pressures: accreditation requirements that emphasise research output and industry relevance, rising class sizes that make teaching load a practical concern, and selection committees that often include external experts who see dozens of similar CVs. A publication list of three Scopus-indexed papers no longer differentiates candidates once the shortlist is formed. What differentiates them is whether the committee can form a concrete picture, after forty minutes of listening, of the candidate’s next paper and of the candidate’s behaviour in an eighty-seat classroom.

The shift is visible in the logistics. Many central and state universities now schedule a twenty-to-thirty-minute research presentation or a short teaching demonstration before the formal interview. Some private universities add a campus visit day that includes lunch with faculty and a meeting with the head of department. Committees use these occasions to test two things the CV cannot show: whether the candidate can explain a contribution without jargon, and whether the candidate’s teaching style is sustainable under Indian classroom constraints. Candidates who arrive prepared only for a formal interview miss the opportunity to shape those impressions.

Indian institutional realities make the signal especially valuable. Teaching loads are high, research support is uneven, and many early-career posts are on contract or probation. A committee that leaves the job talk uncertain about the candidate’s research trajectory is more likely to rank the candidate lower, even if the CV looks strong. Conversely, a clear signal on both research and teaching reduces the risk that the appointment will later be viewed as a mismatch. The job talk and campus visit are therefore not additional hurdles; they are the moments when the candidate can convert a shortlist position into a ranked offer.

Committee composition: Most Indian commerce selection committees include at least one external expert from a research-active department and one internal member who will share the teaching load. Both are looking for different signals; the structure of the talk must address both.

Designing the Job Talk So the Research Contribution Is Visible in the First Eight Minutes

A job talk in an Indian commerce setting is not a conference presentation. The audience is mixed: some members have read the CV, others have not; some work in the candidate’s sub-field, others do not. The contribution must therefore be stated early, in plain language, and then supported by one clear piece of evidence. The recommended structure occupies the first eight minutes with four elements and leaves the remaining time for questions and a short teaching-related bridge.

The opening sentence names the practical problem the paper addresses in an Indian or emerging-economy context. An example is: “Indian banks continue to report higher non-performing assets in the MSME segment than in large-corporate lending, even after the introduction of the GST-based cash-flow assessment.” The second sentence states the paper’s contribution: “This paper shows that a simple modification to the standard credit-scoring model, using GST-return volatility rather than turnover alone, improves prediction accuracy by twelve percentage points in a sample of 4,200 MSME accounts from three private banks.” The third element is the identification or data claim that makes the result credible: “The sample covers the period before and after the GST transition, and the modification is applied only to firms that filed returns in both regimes.” The fourth element is the implication for practice or policy: “The result suggests that banks can reduce false rejections of viable MSMEs without relaxing their existing risk thresholds.”

These four sentences should be written on a single slide or spoken without slides. The remainder of the talk then walks through one table or figure that supports the central result, explains the main robustness check, and stops. Candidates who continue into a full literature review or a second paper lose the attention of the external expert and the internal teaching-load member alike. The goal is not to summarise the entire dissertation or publication record; the goal is to leave every committee member able to restate the contribution in one sentence after the talk ends.

If the department has asked for a teaching demonstration in the same session, the research talk should end with a one-sentence bridge: “The same data set can be used in a third-year elective to show students how a simple volatility measure changes a credit decision.” This bridge signals that the candidate sees the research as teachable material rather than as a separate activity.

Slide discipline: Limit the research portion to six slides maximum. The first slide contains the four sentences above. Subsequent slides show only the main result table, one robustness check, and a limitations slide. Extra slides can be held in reserve for questions.

Turning the Teaching Segment into a Classroom Signal Rather Than a Lecture Rehearsal

When a teaching demonstration is required, the most common mistake is to deliver a shortened version of a regular lecture. Committees have seen many such rehearsals. What they have seen less often is a candidate who, in twelve to fifteen minutes, demonstrates a classroom practice that works under Indian constraints: large class size, mixed preparation, limited technology, and the need to check understanding before the next topic.

A practical structure begins with a one-minute problem that students (or the committee role-playing as students) can attempt immediately. For a financial-accounting topic the problem might be: “A firm records a cash sale of ₹50,000 and a credit sale of ₹30,000. Write the two journal entries and state which entry affects the cash-flow statement.” Committee members are given thirty seconds to write. The candidate then collects two or three responses verbally, identifies the common error (usually the failure to separate the credit sale from the cash-flow statement), and shows the correct entries on a whiteboard or a single projected slide. The segment ends with a one-sentence statement of what the candidate would do next in a full class: “In the remaining forty minutes I would ask students to classify three more transactions and then collect an exit ticket on the cash-flow distinction.”

This structure produces three signals the committee can observe. First, the candidate starts with student work rather than with definitions. Second, the candidate notices and names a common error rather than assuming understanding. Third, the candidate has a plan for the rest of the class that does not require additional preparation time. These signals are more useful to an internal member who will share the teaching load than a polished lecture on the same topic. The external expert also sees that the candidate can translate a research insight (if the bridge was used) or a standard concept into a checkable classroom move.

If the department does not schedule a formal teaching demonstration, the candidate can still create a short teaching signal inside the job talk by reserving the final three minutes for the problem-and-error sequence above. The invitation is explicit: “May I show you, in three minutes, how I would open the same topic with a third-year class?” Most committees accept the invitation because it is time-bounded and interactive.

Managing Informal Campus Interactions Without Over-Promising or Under-Preparing

The campus visit, when it occurs, typically includes a meeting with the head of department, a conversation with two or three faculty members over tea or lunch, and sometimes a brief tour of the department or library. These interactions are not neutral. Faculty members form impressions about whether the candidate will be a colleague who shares the teaching load, contributes to departmental seminars, and does not generate administrative friction. Candidates who treat the visit as purely social, or who use it to negotiate terms, usually leave a weaker impression than candidates who treat it as an opportunity to ask two or three informed questions and to offer one concrete, low-cost contribution.

Before the visit the candidate should read the department’s recent annual report or the university’s latest NAAC self-study section on commerce, if publicly available, and note one specific activity or gap. During the conversation with the head of department the candidate can then ask: “I noticed the department ran three research seminars last year. Is there a slot in the next semester where an early-career faculty member could present a work-in-progress?” The question demonstrates that the candidate has looked at the existing record and is willing to add to it without demanding resources. A parallel question for a faculty colleague might be: “How does the department currently handle the common error in cash-flow classification that I just showed in the teaching segment? Is there a shared exit-ticket practice, or does each faculty member design their own?”

The candidate should also prepare one bounded offer. An example is: “If appointed, I can share the three-artefact placement evidence file I already maintain for guest lectures, so the placement cell has an additional source of student-level notes.” The offer is specific, uses an existing practice, and does not require the department to change its procedures. Offers that require new funding, reduced teaching load in the first year, or changes to the curriculum are better left for the formal negotiation stage after a verbal offer.

Throughout the visit the candidate should avoid two extremes. The first is the detailed discussion of salary, housing, or probation terms; these are handled after the committee ranks the candidates. The second is silence on research and teaching. A candidate who asks only logistical questions leaves the impression that the academic work is secondary. The balance is to spend roughly two-thirds of the informal time on research-pipeline and classroom-practice questions and one-third on practical logistics that affect the ability to do the work (office space, library access, seminar calendar).

Note-taking: Immediately after each informal conversation, record the two or three points the faculty member emphasised. These notes become useful both for the formal interview later the same day and for the thank-you email the following morning.

Answering the Four Committee Questions That Follow a Job Talk

After the job talk and any teaching segment, committees typically ask one or more of four questions. Each can be answered from the structure already presented.

The first is “What is the next paper?” The answer should be a one-sentence extension of the contribution just shown: “The next paper replaces the three-bank sample with a larger set of accounts from the Credit Information Bureau and tests whether the same GST-volatility modification improves prediction outside the original banks.” The answer demonstrates a pipeline rather than a single finished product.

The second is “How will you teach this material?” The answer points back to the teaching segment: “I would open the elective with the same thirty-second problem I just showed, collect the common error on the cash-flow distinction, and then use the bank data set as the live example for the credit-scoring module.” The link between research and teaching is made visible again.

The third is “What support do you need in the first year?” The disciplined answer names one low-cost item already discussed in the campus visit: “Access to the departmental seminar slot I mentioned to the head, and continued use of the library’s electronic resources for the Credit Information Bureau data.” Requests for reduced teaching load or research grants are better deferred until a formal offer is on the table.

The fourth is “Why this department?” The answer should reference one concrete feature observed during the visit or in the public record: “The department already runs a regular seminar series and has a placement cell that records student-level outcomes. Both practices match the way I already document my own work.” The answer shows fit without flattery.

Timing: Keep each answer under ninety seconds. Committees that have already heard a clear contribution in the first eight minutes of the talk are more likely to accept short, concrete replies than long elaborations.

Pitfalls That Dilute a Strong Research or Teaching Signal

Several common practices reduce the value of an otherwise solid job talk and campus visit. The first is opening with a literature review or a personal biography. Both delay the contribution and consume the attention of the external expert. The second is delivering a full conference-length presentation when only twenty minutes have been allocated; the result is that the main finding is rushed or omitted. The third is treating the teaching demonstration as a lecture on definitions rather than as a demonstration of error detection and student work. The fourth is using the campus visit to negotiate salary or to criticise the current curriculum; both create friction before the committee has ranked the candidate. The fifth is failing to write the four-sentence contribution statement in advance. Candidates who improvise the opening under time pressure usually produce a less precise signal than candidates who have rehearsed the four sentences until they can be delivered without notes.

A final pitfall is the absence of any limitation. Committees that contain research-active members expect to hear what the paper cannot claim. A single sentence—“The result is estimated on private-bank accounts and may not generalise to public-sector banks with different MSME portfolios”—protects the contribution from later discounting. Candidates who present only strengths leave the committee to supply the limitations themselves, usually in a less favourable form.

Closing Field Checklist

This week
□ Write the four-sentence contribution statement for the paper you will present and practise delivering it in under ninety seconds without slides.
□ Prepare a twelve-minute teaching segment that begins with a thirty-second student problem, collects a common error, and ends with a one-sentence plan for the rest of the class.
□ Identify one concrete, low-cost contribution you can offer during the campus visit (seminar slot, shared exit-ticket practice, or placement-evidence file).

Before the campus visit
□ Read the department’s most recent public annual report or NAAC section and note one specific activity or gap.
□ Prepare two informed questions: one for the head of department on the seminar calendar, and one for a faculty colleague on a classroom practice.
□ Limit the research slides to six; place extra material in a reserve folder for questions only.

On the day
□ Open the job talk with the four-sentence statement; do not begin with literature or biography.
□ If a teaching demonstration is scheduled, start with student work rather than definitions.
□ During informal conversations, spend two-thirds of the time on research-pipeline and classroom questions and one-third on logistics that affect the ability to do the work.
□ After each conversation, note the two or three points emphasised by the faculty member for use in the formal interview and the next-day thank-you email.
□ Keep every answer to the four standard post-talk questions under ninety seconds and anchored to material already shown.
