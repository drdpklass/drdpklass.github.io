How Indian Scholars Should Read an RBI Financial Inclusion Index Report Before Estimating Regional Credit Access Gaps

Every year the Reserve Bank of India publishes its Financial Inclusion Index. The headline number rises steadily, and state-level ranks appear in presentations, newspaper columns, and, increasingly, empirical papers that try to explain why credit access differs across districts or states. For an Indian finance scholar the temptation is obvious: drop the latest FI-Index score into a regression as a measure of “financial inclusion” and proceed to estimate effects on MSME lending, agricultural credit, or household formalisation. The problem is that the index is a composite policy indicator constructed for monitoring, not a pure outcome variable or a clean treatment intensity. Using it without a careful reading produces coefficients that look precise and say almost nothing that a referee will accept.

This briefing walks through a practical protocol for reading an RBI Financial Inclusion Index report before you estimate regional credit-access gaps. The goal is not to discourage use of the index; it is to force the extraction of what is actually measured, what is imputed, and what must be replaced or instrumented if the paper is to survive review.

Why the FI-Index is not a ready-made regressor

The FI-Index is a weighted composite of three broad dimensions—Access, Usage, and Quality—drawn from multiple administrative and survey sources. Access captures physical and digital points of presence. Usage looks at the intensity of deposit, credit, and insurance products. Quality attempts to measure aspects such as financial literacy, consumer protection, and inequality of access. Because the weights and the underlying indicators change over time and because many components are themselves ratios or indices, the headline score is not a cardinal measure of credit access that can be interpreted in percentage terms.

Scholars who treat the state-level FI score as an independent variable therefore face three immediate problems. First, the index mixes supply-side infrastructure (branch density, ATMs, business correspondents) with demand-side outcomes (accounts opened, credit disbursed). Any regression of “credit outstanding per capita” on the FI-Index is partly regressing a component on itself. Second, the index is published with a lag and is revised; the number available when a paper is written may not be the number that will be cited later. Third, state ranks are sensitive to small changes in a few high-weight indicators, so cross-sectional differences can be driven by measurement rather than by genuine differences in credit access.

Callout. Before you download the Excel file of state scores, open the methodology annex and list every indicator that enters the Access and Usage sub-indices. Circle any indicator that is also present, even under a different name, in your proposed dependent variable. Those circled items cannot stay on the right-hand side.

Anatomy of the RBI Financial Inclusion Index

The current construction follows a three-pillar structure. Access is built largely from supply-side counts: bank branches per 100,000 adults, ATM density, business correspondent outlets, and digital access points. Usage draws on volume and value of deposits, credit accounts, insurance policies, and pension subscribers, often normalised by adult population or by number of accounts. Quality incorporates a smaller set of indicators drawn from surveys (financial literacy scores, grievance redressal ratios) and from administrative data on inactive accounts or zero-balance accounts.

Weights are not fixed across years. When RBI updates the index it publishes a note explaining whether new indicators have been added or whether normalisation has changed. For a scholar this means that a 2024 state score is not automatically comparable with a 2021 score without a bridge series or a statement that the methodology was held constant. The report also releases district-level data for selected years; these are useful but incomplete, and many districts still show missing values for quality indicators.

A practical first step is to construct a one-page map: list the three pillars, the sub-indicators under each, the source (RBI, NPCI, insurance regulators, survey), the year of data, and the weight if disclosed. This map becomes the reference document for every subsequent modelling decision.

Reading the methodology and data sources first

The methodology section is usually short, but the footnotes and the data appendix are long. Read them in this order:

  1. Identify the reference date of each underlying series. Credit outstanding may be as of March, while literacy survey data may be from a year earlier.
  2. Note any imputation or smoothing. Some indicators are carried forward when fresh data are unavailable; others are adjusted for outliers.
  3. Check the adult-population denominator. RBI uses projected adult population; small differences in projection methodology affect density ratios.
  4. Record whether the index is published as a level or as an index number normalised to a base year. Level scores and base-year indices cannot be mixed in a panel without conversion.

Once these facts are written down, the scholar can decide which components are usable as controls and which must be reconstructed from primary sources. For credit-access papers the most common reconstruction is to pull branch density and credit-account density directly from the Basic Statistical Returns rather than relying on the composite Access score.

Mapping index components to a credit-access outcome

Suppose the paper’s outcome is the share of MSMEs that report having an outstanding loan from a formal institution, measured from the Annual Survey of Industries or from a state-level enterprise census. The FI-Index’s Usage pillar contains “credit accounts per 1,000 adults” and “credit outstanding per adult.” Both are mechanically related to the outcome. The correct approach is therefore to exclude the entire Usage pillar or, at minimum, to drop the credit-related indicators and re-estimate a partial index.

Access indicators (branch and BC density) can be retained as supply-side controls, but only after confirming that the density measure is calculated on the same adult-population base used in the paper. Quality indicators are rarely collinear with credit outcomes and can usually stay, provided the scholar notes their survey nature and the limited cross-sectional variation.

A clean mapping table has four columns: (1) proposed right-hand-side variable, (2) exact FI-Index component that overlaps, (3) decision (drop, replace with raw series, keep), and (4) alternative data source if replacement is required. This table belongs in the appendix and is referenced in the identification section.

Callout. If your outcome is any measure of credit volume, number of credit accounts, or formal borrowing, treat the Usage pillar as contaminated. Reconstruct the Access pillar from Basic Statistical Returns and use only that reconstructed series.

In practice the most useful reconstruction begins with the Basic Statistical Returns published by the RBI. Branch density can be recalculated using the same adult-population series the scholar employs elsewhere in the paper, eliminating the denominator mismatch. Business-correspondent numbers are available in the same returns or in the accompanying notes to the FI-Index. Once these two series are in hand, a simple unweighted average or a weighted average that matches the published Access weight produces a clean supply-side measure. The difference between this reconstructed series and the published Access sub-score is itself informative: it reveals how much of the official Access score is driven by indicators the scholar has chosen to exclude.

District-level variation offers an additional check. When RBI releases district FI scores, the scholar can test whether the state-level coefficient survives aggregation to the district. If the relationship disappears at the finer level, the state result is likely an artefact of between-state differences in industrial structure or urbanisation rather than a genuine inclusion effect. Conversely, if the district result is stronger, the paper gains a robustness claim that many referees find persuasive.

A further practical step is to align the FI-Index vintage with the outcome data window. Credit-access measures drawn from the ASI or from state enterprise surveys are usually available with a one- or two-year lag. Using an FI score from the same fiscal year as the outcome creates a mechanical contemporaneous correlation. Lagging the index by one year and, where possible, using the originally published rather than the revised series reduces this problem. The paper should report both the contemporaneous and the lagged specifications.

Five identification traps that appear in Indian state-level papers

State-level FI scores invite several recurring errors.

  1. Simultaneity. States that already have high credit demand attract more branches; the Access score rises because of the credit activity the paper is trying to explain.
  2. Common trends. National programmes (Jan Dhan, Mudra, digital payment incentives) raise both the index and formal credit at the same time. Without year fixed effects and, preferably, programme-specific controls, the coefficient on the FI score absorbs the national policy effect.
  3. Rank versus level. Using the state rank instead of the score discards magnitude information and introduces mechanical negative correlation with other ranked variables.
  4. Missing quality data. Several smaller states have incomplete quality indicators. Dropping them changes the sample; imputing them introduces attenuation. The paper must report both samples.
  5. Revision risk. RBI occasionally revises earlier years when new data arrive. A coefficient estimated on the originally published series may change when the revised series is used. The robustness section should re-estimate on both vintages if available.

Each trap has a practical remedy that can be written into the identification strategy before the first regression is run.

Building a scholar’s extraction and audit table

A usable extraction table contains one row per state-year (or district-year) and the following columns:

  • Raw FI-Index score and rank
  • Access, Usage, and Quality sub-scores
  • List of credit-related indicators included in Usage
  • Reconstructed Access score using only BSR branch and BC data
  • Adult population used by RBI and the alternative population figure used in the paper
  • Flag for any imputed or carried-forward indicator
  • Source document and page or table number

The table is built once and then used for every specification. When a referee asks “did you check that the index does not contain your outcome?” the answer is a reference to this table rather than a new calculation.

Limitations, robustness, and what the index cannot do

The FI-Index cannot serve as an instrument for credit access. It is partly an outcome of the same credit decisions under study. It cannot be interpreted as a continuous treatment intensity whose coefficient has a percentage interpretation. And it cannot replace a careful reading of the underlying administrative data.

Minimum robustness checks are:

  • Re-estimate after dropping the Usage pillar entirely
  • Replace the Access score with raw branch density from BSR
  • Show results with and without the quality component
  • Re-run on the original and revised vintages of the index
  • Add year and state fixed effects and report the within-state variation that remains

If the coefficient of interest disappears once the contaminated components are removed, the paper must either change its claim or abandon the index as a regressor. That is a better outcome than a desk rejection six months later.

Closing checklist for the Policy Analysis Series

Before the first table is produced, confirm each item:

  1. Methodology annex read and every indicator listed.
  2. Any component that overlaps the outcome variable circled and excluded or replaced.
  3. Reference dates and adult-population denominators recorded.
  4. Reconstructed Access series built from Basic Statistical Returns where needed.
  5. Extraction table completed and stored with the replication package.
  6. Five identification traps addressed in the identification section.
  7. Robustness specifications pre-committed in the pre-analysis plan or appendix.
  8. Citation is to the specific RBI report year and, if possible, the exact table or Excel release.

The RBI Financial Inclusion Index is a valuable monitoring tool. Scholars who follow the extraction protocol above produce papers in which the coefficient on any remaining inclusion measure can be interpreted as a supply-side residual rather than a mechanical restatement of the outcome. That distinction is what separates a contribution from a correlation that disappears under the first robustness check. It becomes a usable scholarly variable only after the scholar has stripped out the parts that are mechanically related to the outcome and has documented every remaining choice. That documentation is what turns a convenient state-level number into evidence that can survive a careful referee.
