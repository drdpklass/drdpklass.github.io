Student-Led SDG Clubs in Indian Commerce Faculties: An Evidence Protocol That Separates Attendance from Target Contribution

Indian commerce faculties host student-led clubs that organise awareness sessions, tree-planting drives, financial-literacy camps, plastic-collection events and guest lectures on sustainable business. These clubs appear in annual reports under “SDG outreach” or “student engagement with the Sustainable Development Goals.” When a NAAC peer team, NBA visit or ranking form asks for evidence of contribution to specific targets, the department produces a list of event titles, the number of students who signed the attendance sheet, and photographs. Attendance is not contribution. An event title that contains an SDG keyword is not a mapping. This briefing gives heads of department, club faculty advisers and IQAC coordinators a practical protocol that converts existing student-club activity into a verifiable SDG evidence file without creating a new bureaucratic layer or requiring students to write long reports.

The setting is Indian undergraduate and postgraduate commerce programmes where student clubs operate under limited faculty oversight, where the same students often participate in multiple clubs, and where institutional claims about SDG engagement are increasingly used in ranking submissions and accreditation self-study reports. The protocol uses the documents the faculty already collects—event proposals, attendance sheets, short activity notes and year-end club reports—and adds only the fields that turn an activity into evidence. Evidence is not a count of events or a count of students who attended. It is a means of showing whether the club’s work produced a documented output that a stranger can inspect and that maps cleanly to one primary SDG target.

1. Why student-led clubs generate the weakest SDG evidence on most campuses

Student clubs have three properties that make them attractive for SDG claims and equally easy to overstate. First, they are voluntary, so the department can describe them as student-driven engagement rather than curricular compulsion. Second, they generate visible activity—posters, photographs, social-media posts—that can be assembled quickly for a peer-team visit. Third, the same activity can be described under several targets depending on the keywords chosen. A financial-literacy camp for women in a nearby village can be listed under SDG 5, SDG 8, SDG 1 and SDG 10. The department counts four contributions from one afternoon event.

Three failures follow an attendance-and-keyword approach. First, the denominator is the number of events held or the number of students who signed in. A camp that twenty students attended for two hours is counted even if no baseline information was collected, no material was left with the participants, and no follow-up was recorded. Second, the numerator has no outcome test. The event is finished when the photographs are taken; nothing is recorded about whether the work generated a usable data table, a process note that another club can reuse, a simple before-after observation, or a recommendation that an external organisation actually received. Third, the claim migrates. The departmental annual report lists “42 SDG club events.” The ranking form repeats the number. The peer team opens three sample files and finds attendance sheets and photographs. The room goes quiet.

Working rule. No club activity is entered in the SDG evidence file until the proposal names one primary SDG target, the activity note contains at least one artefact that a stranger can inspect without reading the entire narrative, and the faculty adviser confirms that the artefact was produced by the students. An attendance count is not a row. A keyword in the event title is not a row.

Club evidence sits next to both files. An event that never produces an inspectable artefact is the same error as a programme mapping that never left the syllabus or a final-year project that never left the literature review.

2. Map each club activity to one primary target and one inspectable artefact

A single club activity can touch several targets. Split the mapping at the proposal stage, before the students book the room or print the posters. Different targets create different evidence tests and different risks of over-claiming. The event proposal form already exists in most faculties; it needs only one additional mandatory field: “Primary SDG target (one number only) and one-sentence justification.”

DecisionWhat usually happensMinimum for a live club-activity row
Primary targetAll events tagged “SDG” or listed under several numbersExactly one primary target chosen with a one-sentence justification that a stranger can test against the planned artefact
Secondary targetsAdded to increase the total count of “SDG events”Allowed in a private annex only; never added to the public headline number
Planned artefactLeft as “event will be held and photographs taken”Named on the proposal: a short data table with source and date, a one-page process note that another club can reuse, a simple before-after observation, or a recommendation letter that was sent

Faculty-adviser confirmationSigned only after the eventSigned on the proposal so that the mapping is agreed before the activity begins

Write the primary target on the proposal before the students are allowed to publicise the event. A financial-literacy session for first-time account holders in two nearby villages can map primarily to Target 8.10 (strengthen the capacity of domestic financial institutions to encourage and expand access to banking, insurance and financial services for all). It does not automatically map to every gender or inequalities target. Say that. Means and ends are not the same row.

Do not. Map every club activity that mentions a social or environmental word, and do not map an awareness session that never leaves the lecture hall. An awareness session can be excellent student engagement. It becomes an SDG mapping row only when the students produce an inspectable artefact that links the session to a specific local observation or decision, and that artefact is documented.

Expiry belongs in the register. Activities completed in the reporting year are eligible for that year’s evidence file. Older activities may appear in a cumulative annex if a ranking form asks for a longer window, but they must be labelled by academic year. Backdating a new mapping onto last year’s events to fatten this year’s table is the administrative version of channel stuffing.

3. The four-document protocol that turns club work into verifiable rows

The protocol has four documents. Each document uses a form or note the faculty already collects or can collect in under fifteen minutes. No new software is required. The only addition is three short fields and one mandatory artefact page.

Document 1 – Proposal

Students name one primary target, one-sentence justification, planned artefact and external boundary. Faculty adviser signs. No publicity begins until this form is complete.

Document 2 – Activity note

Within one week of the event the students submit a one-page note that shows the artefact (sample table, draft process note, list of observations). Adviser notes any change in target or boundary.

Document 3 – Artefact page

The activity note contains a dedicated half-page “Evidence Artefact” section that a stranger can read without the rest of the narrative. The artefact is dated and sourced.

Document 4 – Year-end register

Only activities that pass Documents 1–3 enter the public SDG evidence file. The register records target, artefact type, boundary and academic year.

Take a familiar commerce example. A student club proposes a two-hour session on digital onboarding for first-time account holders in one nearby village. Primary target is 8.10. Planned artefact is a simple table of time-to-account-opening and number of documents required before and after the session, collected from the bank branch with permission, plus a one-paragraph process note that the branch can keep. Boundary is “one village, one bank branch, November 2026.” The activity note contains the completed table with source note and date. The faculty adviser confirms the artefact exists and was produced by the students. The activity enters the evidence file as one row under Target 8.10. That is a mapping. It is not a claim that the university expanded financial inclusion in India.

If the only shared object is an attendance sheet and a set of photographs, you have an activity record. Activity records satisfy the student-welfare office. They are not the SDG evidence claim.
Cut-off test. Freeze the cohort of activities on the same date as the academic year ends. A late note submitted after the year closed belongs to next year. Artefacts dated after the cut-off do not move the table.

4. What counts as target contribution versus attendance or activity volume

Indian commerce student clubs often measure success by the number of students who attended and the number of events held. The protocol replaces both measures with a contribution test. Contribution is an artefact that a stranger can inspect and that links the activity to one primary target. Attendance is a participation record. Volume is a count of events. Neither is contribution.

Four artefact types survive inspection. First, a short data table with source, date and a local observation that is not already in a published document. Second, a one-page process note that another club or an external organisation can reuse without rewriting. Third, a simple before-after observation collected by the students (time taken, number of documents, number of participants who opened an account). Fourth, a recommendation letter written by the students and actually sent to an external organisation, with a copy retained. Photographs and attendance sheets support the claim that the activity occurred; they do not replace the artefact.

Separation rule. The headline number of “SDG-aligned club activities” may include only activities that have a confirmed artefact for the same students and the same academic year. Attendance numbers may appear in a private annex for student-welfare reporting. They never appear in the public SDG evidence file.

Student ownership is the anti-ghostwriting rule. The artefact must be produced by the students. A table copied from a bank’s published annual report is not an artefact unless the students add a calculation, a comparison or a local observation that is not already in the published document. A process note written by the students after the event is an artefact. A recommendation letter written by the students and actually sent is an artefact. The faculty adviser’s role is to confirm that the students did the work, not to supply the table.

5. The departmental register and the anti-double-counting rule

The departmental register is a simple spreadsheet or one-page table that lists only the activities that passed the four-document protocol. Columns are academic year, primary target, artefact type, external boundary, number of students involved, and a link or file reference to the artefact page. The register is the only document the department produces when a peer team or ranking form asks for club evidence.

The anti-double-counting rule prevents the same activity from appearing under multiple targets or under multiple clubs. One activity, one primary target, one row. If two clubs jointly organise an event, the row is entered once under the lead club. Secondary targets stay in a private annex. The same students who attend five events are not counted five times in the public file; only the activities that produced artefacts are listed.

Do not. Add the same event under several targets to increase the total number of “SDG contributions.” Do not count the same students multiple times across events. Do not include activities that have photographs but no artefact page.

The register is frozen at the end of the academic year. Later additions require a dated note explaining why the activity was not entered earlier. Backdating is not permitted. The register is retained for the same period as other departmental assessment records so that a peer team can inspect both the summary and the underlying artefacts.

6. The five club-washing errors that fail peer-team inspection

Five errors appear repeatedly when departments present student-club activity as SDG evidence. First, keyword washing: every event whose title contains “sustainable,” “inclusive” or “green” is counted. Second, attendance washing: the number of students who signed in is reported as the contribution. Third, photograph washing: a folder of images is produced in place of an artefact. Fourth, multi-target washing: one afternoon event is listed under four targets. Fifth, ghost-artefact washing: the artefact page is written by the faculty adviser or copied from a published source after the event.

Each error fails for the same reason. The peer team opens the underlying file and finds no inspectable link between the work done and a specific target. The protocol prevents the first four errors by requiring a single primary target and a named artefact on the proposal. It prevents the fifth error by requiring the faculty adviser to confirm that the students produced the artefact. When the protocol is followed, the peer team can open three sample artefact pages and see a table, a process note or a sent letter. The room does not go quiet.

If the only shared object is a title, an attendance sheet and a set of photographs, you have an activity record. Activity records satisfy the student-welfare office. They are not the SDG evidence claim.

7. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this checklist before the next club activity is publicised and before the next ranking or accreditation submission is finalised.

  • Every club activity that will be claimed as SDG evidence has a signed proposal that names exactly one primary target, a one-sentence justification, a planned artefact and an external boundary.
  • The activity note contains a dedicated artefact page that a stranger can read without the rest of the narrative. The artefact is dated and sourced.
  • The faculty adviser has confirmed that the students produced the artefact. Photographs and attendance sheets are retained but are not treated as the artefact.
  • The departmental register lists only activities that passed the four-document protocol. Secondary targets and attendance numbers stay in private annexes.
  • No activity appears under more than one primary target. Joint events are entered once. The same students are not counted multiple times in the public file.
  • The register is frozen at the end of the academic year. Backdating is not permitted. Artefacts dated after the cut-off belong to the next year.
  • Three sample artefact pages can be produced within five minutes when a peer team asks to see the underlying evidence.

Student-led clubs remain a valuable channel for engagement. They become a reliable channel for SDG evidence only when the department replaces attendance counts and keyword lists with a short protocol that produces inspectable artefacts. The protocol uses documents the faculty already collects. It adds three fields and one half-page artefact. The result is a register that survives inspection rather than a folder of photographs that does not.
