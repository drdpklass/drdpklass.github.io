How Indian Finance Scholars Should Write a Literature Review That Editors Cannot Call a Chronology

A literature review that reads like a chronological list of papers is one of the fastest ways to receive a desk reject from a serious finance journal. Editors do not need a history lesson; they need to see that you understand the live debate, that your paper enters that debate at a precise point, and that the gap you claim is real. This brief explains how Indian finance scholars can write a literature review that performs those three jobs, using the data environments and journal expectations that researchers in India and other emerging economies face. The goal is a section that an editor can finish in eight minutes and still know exactly where your contribution sits.

Why Chronological Literature Reviews Trigger Desk Rejects in Indian Finance

Desk editors at journals that Indian finance scholars target—Journal of Corporate Finance, Journal of Banking and Finance, Pacific-Basin Finance Journal, Emerging Markets Review, and the better-ranked Indian outlets—see hundreds of manuscripts a year. Many of those manuscripts open the literature review with “Smith (1995) found X. Jones (1998) found Y. Patel (2003) examined India and found Z.” The editor stops reading. The pattern signals that the author has collected papers rather than joined a conversation. In a high-volume field where identification strategies and data quality vary sharply, a chronology does not tell the editor whether the paper improves on existing evidence or simply adds another coefficient from a new sample.

The problem is especially acute for papers that use Indian data. An editor who sees a long sequence of single-country studies on promoter ownership, bank ownership, or board independence without a clear statement of what remains unresolved will treat the manuscript as incremental. The same editor will keep reading if the review organises the literature by the economic mechanism, the identification threat, or the institutional friction that the new paper addresses. Chronology hides the mechanism; a structured review reveals it.

A second reason is space. Most finance journals now expect the introduction to carry the contribution and the literature review to be tight. A chronological review that runs to six pages forces the editor to hunt for the gap. When the gap is buried after page four, the paper is often returned with a short note that the contribution is unclear. Writing the review so that the gap appears early, and is then justified by the organised literature, shortens the editor’s search and raises the probability that the paper moves to review.

Callout. If your literature review can be summarised as “previous studies found mixed results,” rewrite it. Mixed results are the starting point, not the contribution.

What a Literature Review Is Actually Supposed to Do

A literature review in a finance paper has three jobs. First, it locates the paper inside an active conversation rather than a list of citations. Second, it shows that the author understands the main identification threats and data limitations of that conversation. Third, it makes the gap precise enough that a reviewer can test whether the paper actually fills it. Everything else—historical background, textbook definitions, and long quotations—is usually costly.

The conversation is not “all papers that have ever estimated the effect of X on Y.” It is the set of papers that share the same economic question, institutional setting, or identification problem. For an Indian finance paper on related-party transactions, the conversation includes both the international literature on tunnelling and the Indian literature that uses the disclosure rules under the Companies Act and SEBI LODR. A review that cites only international papers treats the Indian institutional detail as an afterthought. A review that cites only Indian papers looks parochial. The useful review shows where the Indian evidence sits relative to the broader debate and what the Indian setting uniquely allows or prevents.

Identification belongs in the literature review even when the paper has a separate identification section. The review should note which earlier papers rely on cross-sectional variation, which use regulatory shocks, and which face the classic endogeneity problem that ownership and performance are jointly determined. When the new paper claims a better instrument or a cleaner shock, the review must show that earlier attempts were imperfect for specific, stated reasons. Vague claims that “prior work does not address endogeneity” are no longer credible.

Those notes show how to carry the same gap into the introduction and methods; the literature review is where the gap is first justified.

Build the Conversation Map Before You Write a Single Sentence

Before drafting, construct a one-page conversation map. List the three to five strands of the literature that the paper must engage. For each strand, record the central claim, the dominant identification strategy, the main data limitation, and the open question that remains. The map prevents the review from drifting into chronology and forces every citation to earn its place.

A practical map for a paper on bank ownership and lending behaviour in India might contain three strands:

  • Ownership and risk-taking (international): claims that government ownership softens budget constraints; identification mostly cross-sectional or privatisation events; open question on whether the effect operates through credit allocation or through deposit franchise.
  • Indian bank ownership studies: claims that public-sector banks lend more to priority sectors; identification often weak because ownership is persistent and policy mandates change; open question on the role of political cycles versus pure ownership.
  • Credit-channel and transmission papers: claims that ownership affects pass-through of policy rates; identification using monetary-policy shocks; open question on whether ownership interacts with capital ratios in the Indian setting.

With the map in hand, the review can open with the economic question, then show how each strand has approached it, and then state which open question the paper takes up. The citations appear in service of the strands rather than in the order they were published. The map also reveals when a strand is thin. If only two papers address the Indian political-cycle question, the review should say so rather than stretch the literature to look larger than it is.

Keep the map as a working document. When a new paper appears during the revision process, add it to the relevant strand and decide whether it changes the gap. Do not add it simply because it is recent. Recency is not a contribution.

Callout. If a citation does not help the reader understand a strand, a limitation, or the gap, delete it. Volume of citations is not evidence of scholarship.

Three Structures That Survive Editor Scrutiny

Three structures work well for Indian finance papers. The choice depends on the nature of the contribution.

The mechanism structure organises the review by the economic channels through which the independent variable is claimed to affect the outcome. For a paper on promoter pledging and investment, the review might have subsections on the collateral channel, the risk-shifting channel, and the governance channel. Each subsection summarises the evidence for and against the channel, notes the identification used, and ends with what remains unresolved. The paper’s contribution is then located in the channel that is least settled or in the Indian institutional feature that alters the channel’s strength. This structure is especially useful when the paper’s novelty is theoretical or when the empirical design isolates one channel.

The identification structure organises the review by the methods used to address endogeneity. One subsection covers papers that rely on fixed effects and lagged variables, another covers instrumental-variable papers, and a third covers papers that exploit regulatory or policy shocks. The review can then show why the dominant approach in the Indian literature is imperfect for the question at hand and why the new design improves on it. This structure is powerful when the paper’s main claim is methodological.

The institutional structure organises the review by the institutional feature that differs across settings. For papers on related-party transactions, one subsection can cover the U.S. literature under the SEC disclosure regime, another the East-Asian literature after the 1997 crisis, and a third the Indian literature under the Companies Act 2013 and SEBI LODR. The review shows how the Indian disclosure rules create a measurement opportunity or a selection problem that earlier settings did not face. This structure is natural for single-country papers that wish to speak to a broader audience.

Whichever structure is chosen, the review should be no longer than necessary. Most successful papers keep the literature review to three or four pages in the submitted manuscript.

How to State the Gap Without Inventing One

The gap must be real, narrow, and consequential. A real gap is one that a knowledgeable reader can verify by looking at the papers you cite. A narrow gap is one that your design can actually fill. A consequential gap is one whose filling changes a belief that matters for theory, policy, or practice. Statements such as “no study has examined this question in India” fail all three tests if the question has been examined elsewhere and the Indian setting does not alter the mechanism in a theoretically interesting way.

Better gap statements take one of three forms. The first is a measurement gap: “Existing Indian studies rely on an ownership threshold of 20 per cent, yet the Companies Act defines control at 50 per cent and SEBI LODR requires related-party disclosure at lower thresholds. We use the actual disclosure filings to measure transactions that cross the regulatory threshold.” The second is an identification gap: “Prior work instruments promoter ownership with industry-average ownership, which is unlikely to satisfy the exclusion restriction once industry shocks affect both ownership and performance. We exploit the staggered implementation of the SEBI LODR amendment that changed the definition of related parties.” The third is a mechanism gap: “Studies document that pledging is associated with lower investment, yet they do not distinguish whether the effect operates through higher cost of capital or through reduced risk-taking. Our design uses firm-level variation in the collateral value of pledged shares to separate the two channels.”

Each of these statements can be checked against the literature. If the statement cannot be checked, it is not yet a gap; it is a hope. After drafting the gap sentence, return to the conversation map and confirm that the papers in the relevant strand do not already close it. If they do, either narrow the gap or change the paper’s claim.

Avoid the phrase “to the best of our knowledge.” Editors treat it as a signal that the author has not searched carefully. If the search is thorough, the organised review itself demonstrates the knowledge. If a paper is later discovered that closes the gap, the phrase will not protect the manuscript.

Handling the India-Specific Literature Without Isolation

Indian finance has a substantial literature of its own. The task is to engage that literature without isolating the paper from the international conversation. Open each subsection with the broader claim and then show how the Indian evidence modifies or fails to modify it. For example: “The international literature finds that government ownership is associated with higher non-performing loans. Indian studies using the same cross-sectional approach report a similar association. However, the Indian studies do not separate the effect of ownership from the effect of priority-sector mandates that apply only to public-sector banks. Our paper uses the differential application of the mandate after the 2015 revision to isolate the ownership channel.”

This pattern shows respect for the Indian literature, demonstrates knowledge of international benchmarks, and makes the incremental contribution clear. It also prevents the review from becoming a list of Indian papers that no international reviewer will recognise.

When the Indian literature is thin on a particular mechanism, say so. Thinness is not a flaw if the paper supplies the missing evidence; pretending the literature is larger than it is creates a credibility problem when the reviewer searches.

Citation practice matters. Use the same reference style the target journal uses. Include working papers only when they are widely circulated or when they contain the cleanest statement of a method. Prefer published versions when both exist. For Indian regulatory papers, cite the official circular or notification rather than a secondary summary; the earlier Policy Analysis Series notes on reading RBI and SEBI documents explain why.

Callout. A literature review that cites only Indian journals for an international submission, or only international journals for an Indian submission, signals that the author has not located the paper correctly.

Common Failure Modes and How to Pre-empt Them

The literature reviews that editors reject share a short list of defects:

  • Chronological organisation that never states the open question.
  • A gap claim that is either too broad (“no one has studied this in India”) or too narrow to matter.
  • Failure to discuss identification threats that every reviewer in the field already knows.
  • Long quotations or textbook definitions that consume space without advancing the argument.
  • Citations that appear only in the review and are never used again in the methods or discussion.
  • An India-only review in a paper submitted to an international journal, or the reverse.

Pre-empt these by writing the conversation map first, drafting the gap sentence second, and only then writing the review. After the first draft, read the review aloud and stop at the end of each paragraph. Ask whether the paragraph has advanced one of the three jobs: locating the conversation, exposing a limitation, or justifying the gap. If it has not, cut or rewrite it. Finally, give the review to a colleague who works in a neighbouring area of finance and ask them to state, in one sentence, what the paper claims to add. If they cannot, the review has not done its job.

The same discipline applies during revision. When a reviewer asks for additional citations, add them to the relevant strand and show how they affect the gap. Do not simply append them at the end of a paragraph. An appended citation that does not change the argument is visible to the editor and weakens the response letter.

Closing Field Checklist

Before the literature review leaves the desk, confirm the following:

  • The conversation map exists and contains no more than five strands.
  • The review is organised by mechanism, identification, or institution—not by year.
  • The gap sentence is specific, checkable, and appears before the end of the second page of the review.
  • Every major citation is used to support a claim about a strand, a limitation, or the gap.
  • Indian evidence is engaged in relation to the broader literature, not in isolation.
  • Identification threats known to the field are acknowledged even if the paper addresses only some of them.
  • The review is no longer than necessary; three to four pages is usually enough.
  • A colleague outside the immediate co-author team can state the paper’s contribution after reading only the review.
  • The same gap language appears, tightened, in the introduction and in the contribution paragraph.
  • No sentence begins with “Smith (year) found” unless the finding is immediately placed inside a strand.

A literature review that passes this checklist will not, by itself, guarantee acceptance. It will, however, remove one of the most common reasons that otherwise competent Indian finance papers are returned at the desk. The editor will know where the paper sits, what it claims to add, and why the claim is worth a reviewer’s time. That is the only job the section needs to do.
