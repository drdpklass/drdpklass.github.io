How Indian Commerce Faculties Should Map Research Papers to Specific SDG Targets Without Turning a Keyword Match into an Impact Claim

Indian commerce faculties can run a keyword search across faculty papers, count the hits against the seventeen goals, and paste the resulting table into an IQAC file or an impact-ranking form. The table looks complete. The mapping is not. SDG targets are specific statements about outcomes; a paper that mentions “poverty” or “sustainability” in the abstract does not automatically contribute to Target 1.1 or Target 13.2. This briefing is a playbook for department heads, research cells, and IQAC coordinators who must map research output to SDG targets without converting a citation or a keyword into an institutional impact claim.

The setting is Indian higher education after NAAC’s emphasis on research and extension, the NIRF research and innovation categories, and the quieter pressure of Times Higher Education Impact Rankings and similar exercises that invite SDG-tagged publications. The same discipline applies to an affiliating university reviewing a college’s self-study and to a single faculty member preparing a research statement for promotion. Mapping is not public relations. It is a means of showing whether published work addresses a defined target with methods and evidence a stranger can inspect.

1. Why a keyword match is not an SDG mapping

Target language under the SDGs is precise. Target 8.5, for example, concerns full and productive employment and decent work for all women and men. A paper that uses the word “employment” in a labour-market regression does not automatically map to 8.5. The paper may estimate wage differentials, test a policy, or simply describe a data set. Without a sentence that links the research question, the identification strategy, and the finding to the target’s intent, the keyword is decorative.

Three failures follow a pure keyword approach. First, the denominator is undefined. Does every abstract that contains “climate” count equally, regardless of whether the paper measures physical risk, estimates a carbon price, or merely cites a government report? Second, the numerator has no relevance test. A finance paper on board composition that mentions “governance” once can be tagged to SDG 16; a paper that tests whether independent directors improve disclosure of climate-related financial risk is a different object. Third, the claim migrates. The departmental newsletter lists “20 papers on SDGs.” The ranking form repeats the number. The peer team opens one paper and finds no target language, no outcome measure, and no discussion of external validity. The room goes quiet.

Working rule. No SDG mapping table leaves the research cell until every row names one primary target, states the research question in one sentence, and attaches a link or DOI that a stranger can open. A keyword count without that sentence is a prospect list, not a mapping.

A paper that merely cites an SDG number in the introduction is the same error as an MoU that never produced shared activity.

2. Choose the primary target before you choose the narrative

A single paper can touch several goals. Split the mapping before you write the narrative. The split is administrative, not rhetorical. Different targets create different evidence tests and different risks of over-claiming.

DecisionWhat usually happens on an Indian campusMinimum for a live mapping row
Primary targetKeyword search tags the paper to two or three goalsOne primary target chosen by the author or the research cell, with a one-sentence justification that a stranger can test against the abstract and the results section
Secondary targetsListed in a long string to increase the total countAllowed in an annex only; never added to the headline number of “SDG-aligned papers”
Research questionLeft implicitStated in the mapping register in plain language that matches the paper’s own contribution paragraph
Identification or methodOmittedNamed (difference-in-differences, survey instrument, case protocol, etc.) so the reader can see whether the design can speak to the target
External validity or boundaryIgnoredOne sentence on the setting (Indian listed firms, one district, student sample) so the claim cannot be read as universal

Write the primary target on the register before you write the SDG code into any public document. A finance paper that estimates the effect of a state-level skill programme on formal employment of young women can map primarily to Target 8.5 or 5.5; it does not automatically map to every employment-related target. Say that. Means and ends are not the same row.

Do not. Map every paper that cites the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals report, and do not map a literature review that merely catalogues prior work on a topic. Citation of the SDGs is not a research contribution to a target. A literature review can be excellent scholarship. It becomes an SDG mapping row only when the review itself produces a new synthesis that the faculty will use in teaching or policy advice, and that use is documented.

Expiry and dormancy belong in the register. Indian faculties sometimes list papers from 2018 because the keyword still matches. The live file should carry a reporting-year cut-off. Papers published or accepted in the reporting year are eligible. Older papers may appear in a cumulative annex if the ranking form asks for a longer window, but they must be labelled by year. Backdating acceptance to fatten this year’s table is the academic version of channel stuffing.

3. Build a paper–target–evidence–claim ladder

Research mapping evidence has four rungs. The paper is the object: title, authors, outlet, year, DOI or permanent link. The target is the primary SDG target chosen with a justification. The evidence is the specific finding or method detail that supports the link (a coefficient, a qualitative theme, a data source). The claim is the institutional sentence that will appear in the self-study or the ranking form. Most campus files stop at the paper and the keyword and call it a claim. The honest file labels the rung.

Paper

Full citation, DOI or stable link, and the reporting year. A conference abstract without a full paper is not yet a row.

Target

One primary target number and a one-sentence reason that quotes or paraphrases the target language and the paper’s contribution.

Evidence

The specific result or design feature a stranger can check: table number, page, or section. “The paper discusses sustainability” is not evidence.

Claim

The institutional sentence limited to what the paper actually did. “Faculty published research relevant to Target X.Y in setting Z.” Do not write “the university advanced the target.”

Take a familiar commerce example. A faculty member publishes a paper estimating the effect of a state apprenticeship subsidy on formal employment of young men in manufacturing clusters. Activity is the completed paper. Output is the published article with a DOI. The primary target might be 8.5 or 8.6. Evidence is the difference-in-differences estimate on formal employment, with the sample described as registered firms in three states. The claim sentence is: “In 2025 the department published research that estimates the employment effect of a state apprenticeship subsidy on young men in manufacturing clusters, relevant to Target 8.5.” That is a mapping. It is not a claim that the university reduced youth unemployment.

Student dissertations need the same ladder if they are to be included. A master’s thesis that analyses firm-level data is an activity and, if finished and deposited, an output for the student. It is not, by itself, faculty research output unless the supervisor is a co-author on a subsequent paper or the thesis method enters a taught module with board-of-studies approval. List the work package: who holds the data, who has permission to share, and whether any external user received a summary. Citation of the thesis in the department newsletter is not evidence that the paper advanced a target.

If the only shared object is a keyword, a citation count, and a table of seventeen goals, you have a search result. Search results can help discovery. They are not the mapping claim.
Cut-off test. Freeze the paper population on the same date as the annual report. A paper accepted in the photography week after the year closed belongs to next year. Acceptance letters dated after the cut-off do not move the table.

4. Separate student work, faculty research, and institutional impact

Three objects are routinely collapsed. Student projects or dissertations, faculty publications, and institutional impact (changes in policy, practice, or curriculum that the research helped produce) are different. The first may support teaching quality or SDG 4. The second is research output. The third is an outcome that requires additional evidence beyond the paper itself.

Faculty publications belong in the research-mapping register. Student work belongs in a separate teaching or extension register unless the student is a co-author on a peer-reviewed paper. Institutional impact belongs in an outcomes register that cites the paper as one input, not as the impact itself. A paper that is later cited by a state planning board is interesting; the mapping row still describes the paper, and a separate row can describe the citation if the board’s document is public and dated.

Separation rule. The headline number of “SDG-aligned research papers” may include only peer-reviewed or equivalent faculty outputs with a primary target. Student theses, conference presentations without full papers, and institutional outcomes stay in their own columns. Mixing them produces a number that no peer team can reconstruct.

Curriculum change is a common migration path. A paper on sustainable supply chains leads to a new elective. The elective can be excellent teaching. It becomes evidence of research impact only when the board of studies minutes name the paper or the faculty member’s work as the source, and the syllabus is filed. The research-mapping row still describes the paper; the curriculum row describes the change. They are linked, not identical.

5. What ranking forms and peer teams actually test

NIRF, NAAC, and impact-ranking forms ask different questions. NIRF research metrics emphasise publications, citations, and patents; they do not require an SDG tag. NAAC criteria on research and extension look for quality, funding, and consultancy. Impact rankings explicitly invite SDG-tagged outputs and may score the volume or the narrative. A peer team for any of them will open a sample of papers and ask three questions: Can you show me the primary target? Can you point to the result that supports the link? Is this paper faculty research or student work?

Prepare the sample pack before the visit. Choose three papers from the mapping table. For each, place the abstract, the contribution paragraph, the relevant results table, and the one-sentence justification in a single PDF. If the justification cannot be supported by those pages, remove the row. A ranking form that lists forty papers and a sample pack that supports twelve is safer than a form that lists forty and a pack that supports four.

Do not. Send the peer team a spreadsheet of keywords. Send them the sample pack and the register with primary targets. A spreadsheet can be reconstructed by anyone with a search tool; the register cannot.

External validity language matters. Indian commerce research often uses listed-firm samples, bank-branch data, or student surveys. The mapping sentence should name the setting. “Research relevant to Target 13.2 on climate action in Indian listed manufacturing firms” is defensible. “Research that advances global climate action” is not. The first sentence survives a question; the second invites one.

6. Avoid the five mapping errors that produce SDG-washing

Five patterns recur. First, the universal claim: every finance paper is tagged to SDG 8 or 9 because “finance supports growth.” Second, the citation claim: a paper that cites the SDGs in the introduction is counted. Third, the student-faculty collapse: master’s projects are added to the faculty total. Fourth, the outcome migration: the paper is treated as the impact. Fifth, the dormant list: papers older than the reporting window remain in the headline count because they once matched a keyword.

Each pattern can be blocked with the ladder and the separation rule. The universal claim fails the primary-target test. The citation claim fails the evidence rung. The collapse fails the separation rule. The migration fails the claim sentence. The dormant list fails the cut-off test. A research cell that applies all five tests will produce a shorter table. The shorter table is the one that can be defended.

Volume without a primary target is not ambition. It is an invitation for the peer team to sample the weakest row.

7. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this checklist before any SDG mapping table leaves the department or the IQAC.

  • Every row names one primary target and a one-sentence justification.
  • Every row has a DOI or stable link that opens.
  • The evidence rung points to a specific result or design feature, not a keyword.
  • Student work, faculty publications, and institutional outcomes are in separate columns or registers.
  • The reporting-year cut-off is applied and labelled.
  • The claim sentence does not say the university “advanced” or “achieved” the target.
  • A sample pack of three papers exists for peer-team or auditor inspection.
  • Secondary targets appear only in an annex, never in the headline count.
Closing brief. Mapping research to SDG targets is useful when it forces a primary-target choice and an evidence check. It is harmful when it converts a keyword search into an impact claim. Indian commerce faculties already produce papers that speak to employment, climate risk, governance, and firm behaviour. The task is to map them honestly, not to inflate the count. A shorter, inspectable table is better for the institution, for the ranking form, and for the next peer team that opens a paper.

The same discipline that prevents SDG-washing in programme mapping and partnership claims prevents it in research mapping. Start with the paper. Choose one target. Attach the evidence. Write the limited claim. Then stop.
