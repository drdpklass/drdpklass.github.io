How to Run the Case Method in an 80-Seat Indian Commerce Classroom Without Losing the Back Row

The Harvard-style case hour assumes twenty students who have read forty pages, a horseshoe room, and a teacher who can wait out silence. The Indian B.Com. or first-year MBA section has eighty seats bolted to the floor, a public-address system, and students who finished a cost-accounting tutorial ten minutes earlier. When faculty “do a case” in that room, the front two rows debate while the back three photograph the slide. This briefing is a working method for running a genuine decision discussion in that architecture—without pretending the room is Boston and without surrendering the hour to lecture.

The method is written for commerce and management teachers in India and other emerging-economy campuses: large sections, mixed English fluency, first-generation writers, and an assessment calendar that still treats internals as mid-term memory tests. It travels to any hall where the case is assigned because NAAC or the programme brochure likes the word “experiential,” and where the teacher then fills the silence with the answer they already know.

1. Why the imported seminar case dies in an 80-seat hall

Three mismatches kill the hour. First, packet length. A 28-page HBS-style narrative with six exhibits is a weekend assignment for a full-time MBA. It is not a Tuesday-night assignment for a student who shares a phone, commutes ninety minutes, and has two other internals. Students skim the first two pages and wait for the teacher to narrate the rest. Second, room geometry. Cold-calling works when faces are visible and names are known. In a raked hall with a single aisle, the teacher can see twelve people. The rest become an audience. Third, status. First-generation students treat a wrong public answer as a social injury. They will not volunteer a ratio if they think the room will laugh at the English around the ratio.

Faculty then compensate in the worst possible way. They project a twelve-slide “case discussion” that already contains the decision, the alternatives and the recommended number. That is a lecture with a case title. The students who prepared feel punished. The students who did not prepare are confirmed. The back row learns that attendance is the only deliverable.

Working rule. A case hour in a large Indian section is successful if at least one number is contested on the board, at least one alternative is rejected in public, and at least eight surnames are attached to claims. If those three things do not happen, you taught a story, not a case.

Do not wait for a new building. Redesign the hour for the room you have: shorter packet, named roles by row, a board that records student language, and a mark that cannot be earned by sitting still.

2. Design the packet so first-generation students can prepare

Preparation failure is usually a design failure. Assign a packet a first-generation student can finish in seventy-five minutes on a bus. That means eight to twelve pages of narrative, two exhibits with units visible, and a one-page assignment sheet with three questions that already name the decision.

2.1 The assignment sheet, not the novel

Print three questions on a separate page and collect that page at the door, or require a photo upload the night before. The questions should force a number and a choice:

  1. What is the decision the protagonist must take this quarter? One sentence.
  2. Which one figure in Exhibit 2 most threatens that decision? Name the cell and the unit.
  3. Which alternative will you reject first, and on what number?

That sheet is the ticket. Students without it sit in a designated overflow row and listen. They are not shamed; they are not called. After two sessions the preparation rate rises because the cost of free-riding is visible and small, not theatrical.

2.2 Local cases beat imported prestige

A listed mid-cap with a BRSR page, a working-capital squeeze, and a regional language newspaper story will out-teach a polished overseas case the class cannot locate on a map. Students can open the annual report on their phones. First-generation students recognise the firm’s product. The discussion can move from “what happened in the packet” to “what the 2025 notes actually say.” Prestige cases still have a place in an elective of thirty. They are a poor default for Financial Management I in a hall of eighty.

Do not. Assign a 40-page case on Friday for a Monday 9 a.m. class and then complain that “Indian students do not prepare.” You designed an impossible task and then moralised the result.

3. Open with a decision, not a recap

The first four minutes decide whether the back row opens a notebook or a shopping app. Do not recap the plot. The plot is in the packet. Write the decision on the board before you speak: “Accept the vendor’s 60-day terms or lose the festive-season order.” Then ask a named student in row four for a yes or no and one number. If you start with “Who would like to summarise the case?”, you have invited the most fluent student to lecture on your behalf for twelve minutes. The rest of the room settles.

Keep a visible clock. Forty-five minutes of discussion, ten minutes of synthesis, five minutes of the take-home computation. If the hour has no clock, the teacher will fill leftover silence with a mini-lecture that undoes the method.

The opening sentence should contain a verb a manager could execute this quarter. If your opening sentence contains “challenges,” “scenario,” or “in today’s world,” you have not opened a case. You have opened a seminar on atmosphere.

4. Map the room so the back row is a role, not a hiding place

Assign standing roles by geography, not by personality. Rotate them each week so no row becomes the entertainment section.

ZoneRole for this hourWhat they must produce
Rows 1–2Protagonist benchState the decision and defend one number from Exhibit 1.
Rows 3–4CFO benchAttack the number. Name the assumption that breaks it.
Rows 5–6Lender / auditor benchAsk what is missing from the notes or the cash cycle.
Last two rowsBoard benchAfter 25 minutes, vote and give one condition they would attach.

Print the map on the assignment sheet. Students walking into the last row know they will speak at minute twenty-five. That is enough notice to stay in the room mentally. A back-row student who must attach a covenant cannot treat the hour as a rest period.

For first-generation students, roles reduce the terror of “say something intelligent.” They are not being asked to perform personality. They are being asked to occupy an office. Offices have scripts. Scripts can be prepared on the bus.

Do. Walk the aisle twice. Stand next to the last row when you ask the board bench to vote. Physical proximity is a participation technology. Remaining at the lectern is how the back row disappears.

5. Use the board as a public ledger, not a slide dump

Slides freeze the teacher’s pre-decision. A board records the class’s fight. Divide the board into four columns before class and refuse to erase student language until the vote.

  1. Decision. The sentence from the opening.
  2. Numbers in play. Only figures students have spoken, with units and the speaker’s surname.
  3. Assumptions. What must be true for the recommended number to hold.
  4. Rejected alternative. One option the class killed, and why.

Write slowly. The act of writing a student’s sentence in public is the reward that makes the next student speak. Correct the unit, not the accent. If the current ratio is stated as 1.4 “times” rather than a pure number, fix the unit on the board and thank the speaker for the exhibit cell. That is how you teach precision without turning the hour into an English viva.

At minute forty, take a photograph of the board and post it to the course group. That photograph is the official note. Students who want a polished summary can write one for the internal. They should not receive a slide deck that makes attendance optional.

Board earns its keep when

A surname sits next to a figure; an assumption is labelled as untested; a rejected option remains visible so the vote cannot pretend consensus.

Board has failed when

Only the teacher’s handwriting appears; the columns are headings with no numbers; the slide is re-projected “for completeness.”

6. Cold-call without humiliation and without a fan club

Random calling is the only way an 80-seat room stays honest. A volunteer-only hour becomes a conversation among six people who already speak English at home. Structured cold-call is not cruelty if the question is bounded and the student had a ticket sheet.

6.1 The bounded question

Never cold-call “What do you think?” Call a cell: “Priya, Exhibit 2, inventory days—higher or lower than last year?” A binary plus a cell can be answered by a quiet student. Follow with “What would have to be true for that rise to be healthy?” only after the first answer is on the board. Stacking two open questions is how you produce panic and then a mumbled “I agree with him.”

6.2 The name file

Keep a printed roster with a tick grid: prepared ticket, spoken, number offered, assumption challenged. Aim for eight new surnames an hour, not the same four discussants. Tell the class in week one that the grid exists and that silence for four consecutive case hours will move the participation band. Then keep the promise. Mystery marking is how students conclude participation is charm.

6.3 Repair after a weak answer

When an answer is wrong, relocate it rather than crush it. “The 45 days is from receivables, not inventory—look at the row title. Take thirty seconds and give me the inventory line.” The room learns that error is editable. First-generation students will risk a number only if they have seen an error survive.

Do not. Use sarcasm, delayed silence after a name, or “can someone help him.” Those moves train the hall that speaking is a status contest. The method needs more speakers, not funnier ones.

Permit one short vernacular clause if it unlocks the number, then ask the student to restate the number in examination English. Fluency is a course outcome. It is not a gate that must be cleared before thought is allowed in the room.

7. Grade the case hour so participation is not theatre

If the internal is still a 20-mark memory test, students will rationally skip preparation. Attach five to eight marks of the course to the case process, and make the rubric public.

EvidenceMarks logicWhat you refuse to count
Ticket sheet (decision + one figure + one rejection)Binary: present and usable, or notLength, handwriting beauty, copied plot summary
Spoken claim with a number or a named assumptionOne tick per hour; cap at four hoursAgreement with the previous speaker
Forty-minute rewrite of the rejected alternativeOne paragraph, one number, due next classA new five-page “report” generated overnight

The rewrite is the learning engine. After the vote, assign: “Write the paragraph that kills the option the class rejected, using the board number.” Collect that paragraph, not a polished memo. Mark it against one criterion—does it use the class number and a unit. That is how the hour becomes writing practice without doubling the Sunday load. It also pairs cleanly with the earlier Teaching Series briefing on feedback first-generation students will actually use.

Protect integrity without a tribunal. If a ticket sheet arrives with sudden polish and no exhibit cell, ask the student in the next hour to point to the cell on a projected exhibit. You are testing location, not running a hearing. Students who cannot find the cell were never in the packet.

Load note. An 80-student section with a ticket sheet and a one-paragraph rewrite is markable in two evenings if you refuse to annotate the whole packet. Capacity is part of pedagogy. A method that requires a tutorial culture you do not have will be abandoned by week five.

8. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this list the night before a case hour. If more than two boxes are empty, teach a problem set instead and save the case for a week when the design is ready.

  • Packet is under twelve pages plus two exhibits; assignment sheet has three bounded questions.
  • Decision sentence is written in advance; it contains a verb a manager could execute this quarter.
  • Room map assigns roles by row; last rows vote with a condition, not with applause.
  • Board has four columns; surnames will sit next to numbers; no answer slide will be projected mid-discussion.
  • Cold-call list names eight students who have not spoken in the last two case hours; questions point at exhibit cells.
  • Ticket sheet is the door pass; rewrite is one paragraph due next class; rubric is already on the question paper.
  • You will walk the aisle twice. You will not fill silence with the recommended answer before minute forty.

The case method is not a personality. It is a sequence: a short packet, a public decision, a contested number, a rejected alternative, a named speaker from the back, and a paragraph that uses the board. Indian commerce classrooms can run that sequence in bolted seats. They cannot run a nostalgia for a seminar that was never staffed here.
