How Early-Career Commerce Faculty Should Design a Teaching Demonstration That an Indian Selection Committee Cannot Ignore

Most early-career candidates spend weeks polishing a research talk and ten minutes sketching a teaching demonstration. Indian selection committees reverse the weighting. In commerce departments the demonstration is often the only live evidence that the candidate can hold an 80-seat room, work with first-generation students, and recover when the plan collapses. This briefing gives a practical design protocol so that the twenty minutes become evidence rather than theatre.

Why the Teaching Demonstration Decides More Offers Than the Research Talk

Research papers can be read offline. A teaching demonstration cannot. Committees see, in real time, whether the candidate can explain a concept without drowning students in jargon, whether they notice who has not spoken, and whether they can change course when the example fails. In Indian commerce programmes the demonstration is frequently conducted in an actual classroom or a seminar hall filled with final-year students who have not been coached. Silence is not neutral; it is data. Many committees also invite one or two final-year students to sit as informal observers; their later comments about clarity and approachability reach the chair before the formal decision.

Departments that hire primarily for teaching load treat the demonstration as the decisive filter. Even research-oriented departments use it to test whether the candidate will survive the first semester without constant rescue by seniors. Candidates who treat the demonstration as a shortened lecture lose offers that the research talk alone would have secured. In practice, a strong demonstration can compensate for a thinner publication list when the department’s immediate need is coverage of large core papers; a weak demonstration rarely compensates for anything.

Practical note: Ask the host, before you travel, for the class strength, the language mix, whether the session will be recorded, and how far the students have progressed in the topic. The act of asking is itself observed. If the host cannot supply the information, plan for an 80-seat mixed-language room that has not yet reached the chapter.

What Indian Selection Committees Actually Watch For

Committees rarely score content coverage. They score three observable behaviours.

First, relationship in the opening minutes. Do you learn two or three names and use them? Do you move, even briefly, away from the podium? Do you respond to a student comment rather than to your own next slide? In rooms where students sit in fixed groups, acknowledging a group rather than an individual still counts as relational work.

Second, checking for understanding rather than for attention. A raised hand that answers “yes, clear” is not evidence. A short pair task whose product you sample is evidence. Committees notice whether you wait after a question and whether you follow a wrong answer instead of moving on. They also notice whether you sample both a confident front-row response and a quieter response from further back.

Third, visible adaptation. When the planned activity stalls, do you change the task, lower the cognitive demand, or invite a different mode of response? Recovery is scored higher than a polished delivery that never meets resistance. Adaptation includes switching from English-only instructions to a bilingual restatement when the first version produces blank faces.

Language mix and prior knowledge are part of the test. In many Indian commerce classrooms a substantial fraction of students are more comfortable in Hindi or a regional language when discussing numbers. Candidates who insist on unbroken English without scaffolding lose points even if the English is excellent. Committees also watch whether you treat a student who answers in Hindi as a full participant or as a temporary exception.

Choosing a Topic That Fits an 80-Seat Commerce Classroom

Choose a topic that can be taught in twenty minutes to students who have not prepared. Ideal candidates are concepts that appear early in a core paper and that contain a common misconception. Examples that work: the difference between cash and accrual in a simple service business; why a firm with positive accounting profit can still face cash trouble; the basic logic of a credit period decision; or a single ratio that reveals a financing risk. A second useful family is the distinction between a journal entry and its effect on the cash book when settlement occurs later.

Avoid topics that require lengthy institutional background or that cannot be illustrated with numbers on a board. Avoid your research topic unless it can be stripped to a classroom-ready core that first-year students can follow. Committees prefer evidence that you can teach the students they actually have, not the students you wish you had. If the department has signalled a heavy teaching load in financial accounting or management accounting, choose a topic from that paper rather than from a specialised elective.

Confirm the syllabus stage if possible. If the class has not yet reached the chapter, treat the demonstration as an introduction that ends with a clear next step. If the class has already covered it, treat the demonstration as a diagnostic that surfaces and corrects a misconception. Both are legitimate; neither requires you to “finish the chapter.” In either case, prepare one extra numerical variation so that you can raise or lower difficulty in the room without redesigning the sequence.

Rule of thumb: Plan content for two-thirds of the allotted time. The remaining third is for student talk, sampling, and recovery. If the host indicates a weaker cohort, plan content for half the time.

Designing the Sequence: From Hook to Evidence of Learning

A workable twenty-minute sequence has five moves.

Hook (2 minutes). Open with a concrete, local situation. A small trader who receives an advance but has not yet delivered the service; a student who sees positive profit but empty bank balance; a supplier who offers 30 days credit versus 2 percent cash discount. State the learning goal in one sentence: “By the end of these twenty minutes you will be able to show, with numbers, why the cash position can diverge from the profit figure.” Write the goal on the board so that late arrivals can still see it.

Activate prior knowledge (3 minutes). Ask a closed question that almost everyone can answer, then one open question. Sample two responses aloud. This surfaces language and prior-knowledge differences without humiliation. If the first open question produces only silence, rephrase it as a choice between two visible options on the board.

Core explanation with board work (6 minutes). Keep the explanation to one core mechanism. Write the numbers yourself. Narrate each step. Pause after the critical line and ask a student to restate it in their own words. Do not advance until at least one restatement is accurate or has been corrected in public. Leave the key calculation visible; do not erase it when you move to the next line.

Pair task (6 minutes). Give a second, slightly varied numerical case. Students work in pairs for three minutes. Circulate, listen, and note two pairs whose work you will sample. Sampling is not optional; it is the evidence the committee is watching for. While circulating, listen for the language students use with each other; that language often reveals whether the core idea has landed.

Close and bridge (3 minutes). Restate the learning goal. Ask one student to name the condition under which the divergence disappears. Point to the next syllabus item. Thank the class by name if you learned any. End while the board still shows the corrected calculation so that the last visual is the learning, not a blank screen.

This sequence can be run with a board and a single handout. Slides are optional and often slower. If you do use slides, limit them to the two numerical cases and the learning goal; everything else belongs on the board or in speech.

Handling the Back Row, Language Mix, and Technology Failure

In an 80-seat room the back row is not decorative. Walk at least once to the third or fourth row while pairs work. Address a question to a student who has not spoken. If the response comes in Hindi or a regional language, restate the substance in English and invite the student to confirm. This signals that content, not medium, is the criterion. In rooms with fixed benches, a short walk down the aisle is enough; you do not need to reach the last row to be seen as present.

Technology failure is common. Projector bulbs die, Wi-Fi drops, and the clicker does not pair. Prepare a fully board-based version of the same sequence. If the projector fails in the first minute, move to the board without apology beyond one sentence. Committees score the recovery, not the equipment. Keep a spare marker and a printed copy of the numerical cases in your pocket so that the switch takes less than thirty seconds.

When a student offers a wrong but common answer, treat it as data. “That is the calculation many of us first try. Let us see where the cash leaves the picture.” The correction is public, the student is not named as wrong, and the class sees the misconception resolved. If the same error appears in two pairs, pause the whole class and correct it once rather than correcting each pair privately.

Inclusion move: Offer the pair task in two versions—one with smaller numbers for students who need more scaffolding. Let pairs choose. The choice itself is evidence of differentiation. Label the versions A and B on the handout so that you can see at a glance which pairs chose which.

Preparing the Materials Packet and Surviving the Questions

Prepare a one-page materials packet for the committee: learning goal, sequence with timings, the two numerical cases, and the exact question you will use to check understanding. Print six copies. Hand them to the chair before you begin. The packet shows that the demonstration was designed, not improvised. Include a one-line note on what you will do if the group is weaker or stronger than expected; this pre-empts the most common follow-up question.

After the demonstration the committee will ask why you chose that topic, what you would do differently with a weaker group, and how the same idea would scale to a full lecture. Answer with the constraints you observed in the room, not with ideal conditions. “With this group the pair task worked because the numbers were small; with a group that has not yet seen the balance-sheet equation I would add a three-minute board warm-up.” If a student observer is present, address one sentence of the answer to the room as well as to the committee.

If asked about research integration, give one concrete sentence: “In week eight I use the same cash-accrual divergence to introduce the working-capital cycle that appears in my paper on trade credit.” Do not deliver a second research talk. If asked about assessment, describe one low-stakes item you would set the following week that re-uses the same numerical structure. Committees are listening for evidence that the twenty minutes connect to the rest of the course rather than standing alone.

Common Failure Modes and How to Recover

The most common failure is over-preparation of content and under-preparation of interaction. Candidates arrive with twelve slides and leave with two unused. Cut to the minimum that still produces evidence of learning. A second common failure is speaking continuously for the first twelve minutes; once the monologue habit is established it is hard to recover student voice.

The second failure is treating silence as consent. If no one answers, wait, then rephrase, then offer a binary choice, then invite a pair to confer for thirty seconds. The wait is visible and scored. Count the seconds silently; most candidates resume talking after three seconds, which is too soon for students who are translating the question.

The third failure is defending the original plan after it has failed. When the example does not land, abandon it. “This illustration is not helping. Let me try a smaller case.” Adaptation is the behaviour committees reward most consistently. A fourth failure is apologising for the room, the time of day, or the students’ apparent tiredness; committees hear the apology as a signal that the candidate expects external conditions to do the teaching work.

Practice the sequence twice with colleagues who will deliberately give wrong answers and ask off-topic questions. Record one run on a phone so you can hear whether your instructions are clear without the slides. In the second practice, ask one colleague to answer only in Hindi so that you rehearse the bilingual restatement under mild pressure.

Closing Field Checklist

Before you travel
□ Confirm class size, language mix, and syllabus stage.
□ Choose a topic that produces a visible misconception and a numerical correction.
□ Prepare a board-only version of the entire sequence.
□ Print the one-page materials packet (six copies).
□ Rehearse once with deliberate interruptions.
In the room
□ Learn and use two names in the first five minutes.
□ State the learning goal in one sentence.
□ Sample at least two pair products aloud.
□ Recover visibly if the plan stalls.
□ Close by restating what students can now do.
