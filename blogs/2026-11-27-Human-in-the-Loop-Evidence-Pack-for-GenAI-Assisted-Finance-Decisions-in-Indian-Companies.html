Human-in-the-Loop Evidence Pack for GenAI-Assisted Finance Decisions in Indian Companies

SEBI’s recent statements on AI guidelines for capital markets, the emphasis on human-in-the-loop oversight, and the RBI’s FREE-AI framework plus draft model-risk guidance have shifted the conversation. Indian finance teams can no longer treat generative AI as a productivity experiment that lives in private chat windows. When a tool drafts a variance narrative, suggests a provision commentary, ranks working-capital exceptions, or proposes disclosure language that later appears in a board pack or statutory filing, the question that auditors, rating agencies and regulators will ask is no longer “did you use AI?” It is “show me the human who reviewed it, the evidence of that review, and the boundary the human was allowed to see.”

A human-in-the-loop evidence pack answers that question with a short, repeatable artefact. It does not require every prompt to be pre-approved by the board. It requires that every GenAI output that can influence a number, a narrative, or a decision that reaches the controller, the audit committee or an external party leaves a dated, attributable human trace. For controllers who must keep the monthly close moving while tools proliferate, the pack is the practical bridge between the principle of human oversight and the evidence that an auditor can re-perform.

Why human-in-the-loop is moving from principle to evidence requirement

Two regulatory threads converged in late 2026. SEBI has signalled detailed guidelines that will require disclosures, accountability of regulated entities, and a human-in-the-loop approach for AI applications in securities markets. The RBI’s FREE-AI committee report (August 2025) and the subsequent draft Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management treat generative and third-party AI models as subject to the same inventory, validation and accountability expectations that already apply to credit and market models. Both threads share one operational implication: the deploying entity remains responsible for the output, regardless of whether the model was built in-house or purchased as a SaaS feature.

In practice this means the “human in the loop” cannot be a policy sentence that says “all AI outputs are reviewed.” It must be a set of artefacts that show, for a sampled period, which human saw which output, against which source data, under which version of the tool, and what decision (accept, edit, reject) was recorded. Without those artefacts the control is untestable. With them, internal audit can re-perform a sample in under an hour and the controller can answer a lender or rating-agency query without reconstructing chat histories.

Controller’s test: If you cannot, within fifteen minutes, pull the evidence pack for any GenAI-assisted paragraph that appeared in last month’s board pack and name the reviewer, the data boundary and the tool version, the human-in-the-loop control does not yet exist.

The shift is especially sharp for listed companies and for banks or NBFCs that already sit under RBI model-risk expectations. Even for unlisted operating companies the same logic applies once institutional lenders or private-equity investors begin asking for process assurance on the numbers they rely on.

Which finance decisions actually need documented human review

Not every AI interaction requires a full evidence pack. The threshold is the same one used for model cards: if the output can appear, even after editing, in a document reviewed by the board, audit committee, statutory auditor, lender or regulator, or if it influences a journal entry, a provision, a covenant calculation or a disclosure, it is in scope. Typical candidates in Indian finance teams include:

  • Variance and bridge narratives that explain movements in EBITDA, working capital or cash flow for the monthly pack.
  • Provision and ECL commentary that links model outputs to management judgement.
  • Disclosure drafting assistants that propose Ind AS or BRSR wording from internal data.
  • Exception ranking tools that prioritise working-capital or inter-company items for the close checklist.
  • Forecast commentary layers that sit on top of an existing driver model and produce the narrative that accompanies the numbers.
  • Subsidiary or segment pack summarisers that compress regional packs into group-level notes.

Tools that only format, translate or spell-check remain outside the full pack. The practical rule is: if a reasonable person could believe the paragraph or the ranking was produced by a human who had access to the ledger, the tool is in scope and needs a human review trace.

Minimum contents of the evidence pack

A usable evidence pack fits on one to two pages per tool (or per major use case) and is written so that an internal auditor who has never seen the tool can understand the risk and re-perform the test. The minimum sections are:

  1. Use-case boundary. Exact purpose (for example, “draft first-pass variance narrative for operating segments”) and explicit non-purpose (for example, “must not propose or post journal entries”).
  2. Data and prompt boundary. Sources the tool may see, sources it must not see, and the current prompt-library or configuration version.
  3. Named human reviewer. Role and, where possible, named individual who must accept, edit or reject the output before it leaves the controlled folder.
  4. Review evidence standard. What constitutes acceptable evidence of review (dated comment in the pack, tracked change with initials, or a short sign-off log). A screenshot of a chat window is not sufficient once the window has scrolled.
  5. Materiality and escalation. Thresholds below which a lighter review is acceptable and the rule for items above the threshold (controller’s own read).
  6. Residual risk and compensating control. What the tool still gets wrong and the extra human step that covers it.
  7. Retention pointer. Where the original AI output, the source export and the human review record are stored for the statutory retention period.

Indian teams that have piloted this find that writing the “non-purpose” and “data boundary” sections first prevents most scope creep. Once those two sentences exist, the temptation to let the tool “just quickly look at the raw ledger” becomes visible and stoppable.

Practical tip: Store the evidence-pack template in the same controlled folder as the monthly reporting calendar. Require a one-line citation of the pack version on the first page of every AI-assisted management pack. The citation itself becomes the starting point for any later audit sample.

The review protocol that produces usable evidence

An evidence pack without a short, repeatable review step is only documentation. The protocol that works in Indian close cycles has four checks that the reporting analyst or reviewer can complete before the controller sees the draft:

  1. Source lock. Every quantitative claim or ranking must be traceable to a cell or line in the approved source export. The reviewer highlights the source; the tool is not allowed to invent a figure.
  2. Boundary fence. The output is compared against the approved data and prompt boundary. Any reference to a data item or logic that was not in the allowed set is rejected, even if the sentence looks fluent.
  3. Direction and consistency check. If the tool attributes a movement to mix or to a specific driver, the reviewer confirms the underlying data moved in the claimed direction and that the story is consistent with the prior period’s accepted narrative.
  4. Decision record. The reviewer records accept / edit / reject with a date and initials (or a tracked comment). Edited text is kept alongside the original AI output so the delta is visible.

These four checks take 10–20 minutes for a typical monthly narrative. They catch the two failure modes auditors worry about most: hallucinated numbers and quiet expansion of scope. Because the checks are listed on the evidence pack, the auditor can re-perform a sample without having to reverse-engineer the process from chat logs.

Logging, versioning and retention that survive an audit

The evidence is only useful if it can still be found six or twelve months later. The practical mechanism used by controllers who have made this work is a simple three-file set for each AI-assisted pack:

  • The original AI output (exported or copied at the moment of generation, with tool version and timestamp).
  • The source export or approved data file that was allowed to be seen.
  • The human review record (tracked changes, comment log or short sign-off sheet) that shows the decision and any edits.

These three files live in a controlled folder named by period and tool version. A one-line change log on the evidence pack itself records every material change to prompt, data boundary or review rule. Version numbers stay simple: v1.0 at first approval, then v1.1 for minor prompt tweaks, v2.0 for a change in data boundary or underlying model. When the statutory auditor samples the March pack, the controller can open the folder, point to the three files, and show the change-log entries that occurred between December and March.

Retention follows the same period already used for management accounts and working papers—normally eight years for companies under the Companies Act. The key is that the AI output and the human review record are retained together; one without the other is incomplete.

How controllers, internal audit and the board use the pack

The controller’s role is to ensure the packs exist, are current, and were actually used. Internal audit does not need to become an AI expert. It needs to test whether the packs exist, whether they are current, and whether the review steps listed on them were performed. A short audit programme that works is:

  • Select three AI-assisted packs from the last quarter.
  • Confirm the evidence-pack version cited in the pack matches the pack stored in the controlled folder.
  • Re-perform the source-lock and boundary-fence checks on two paragraphs or rankings.
  • Confirm that the human review record exists, is dated, and shows a decision.
  • Check that the change log contains every prompt or boundary edit that occurred in the period.

For the board or audit committee, the controller’s quarterly AI update can be reduced to a single page: number of in-scope use cases, number of current evidence packs, any use case that failed a review test and was temporarily withdrawn, and any residual risk that has been accepted in writing. This keeps the conversation at the level of control design rather than prompt engineering and aligns with the human-in-the-loop expectations now being signalled by both SEBI and the RBI.

A 60-day rollout that fits the Indian close calendar

Day 1–10: Inventory. List every GenAI use that has already produced text or rankings for a management or board pack. Do not start with a perfect policy; start with the tools that are already in use.

Day 11–25: Write the first two or three evidence packs. Choose the highest-volume or highest-materiality use cases. Use the seven-section template. Have the current prompt owner and the reporting manager co-sign the first version.

Day 26–40: Embed the four-check protocol. Add the checks to the monthly close checklist. Run them on two live packs and record the time taken and the errors caught. Adjust the materiality threshold if the tests are catching too much or too little.

Day 41–60: Hand the process to internal audit for a dry-run review. Fix the gaps they find. From the following month the evidence packs are simply part of the normal close.

Teams that follow this sequence usually discover that two or three use cases account for most of the risk, and that formalising them reduces rather than increases the controller’s personal review time, because the obvious errors are caught earlier by the analyst.

Common failure modes in emerging-market finance teams

Several patterns show up quickly in Indian and other emerging-market finance teams. Naming them in advance reduces surprise.

  • Shared login, no named reviewer. A single corporate seat is used by five people. The evidence pack must name a single reviewer role even if multiple people type prompts; the review record must still be attributable.
  • Excel paste culture. Outputs are pasted into spreadsheets that then become the “source.” The pack must require that the original AI output and the source export remain linked, not replaced by the paste.
  • Chat-window evidence. Teams rely on a screenshot of a conversation that has already scrolled. The protocol must force an export or copy of the output at the moment of generation plus a separate dated review record.
  • Vendor black box. Some SaaS tools do not expose the prompt or the model version. In that case the pack records the vendor name, the contract clause that limits data use, and the output-level review tests that can still be performed. The residual risk is higher and should be accepted explicitly by the controller.
  • Year-end and festival compression. In March and during peak festival weeks the pressure to “just generate the draft” rises. The review protocol must be short enough that it survives the peak; otherwise it will be bypassed exactly when the risk is highest.
  • Subsidiary language drift. Regional teams start using local-language prompts. The data-boundary and review rules still apply; the pack should note the language and require a bilingual reviewer for material items.
Field checklist for controllers
  • Every GenAI use case that can influence a board or audit pack has a current evidence pack.
  • The pack names a single reviewer role, a data and prompt boundary, and a clear review-evidence standard.
  • The four-check protocol is on the monthly close checklist and was run this period.
  • Original AI output, source export and human review record are retained together in a controlled folder.
  • The change log shows every prompt or boundary change since the last audit.
  • Internal audit has a one-page programme that can re-perform the checks on a sample.
  • Residual risks listed on the packs have been accepted in writing by the controller.

A human-in-the-loop evidence pack does not make GenAI safe by itself. It makes the human review step visible, attributable and testable. For Indian controllers who must keep the monthly pack moving while SEBI, the RBI and institutional investors raise the bar on process assurance, that visibility is the difference between an experiment that can be defended and an experiment that cannot.
