How Indian Commerce Faculties Should Design an SDG-Aligned Final-Year Project Protocol That Produces Verifiable Evidence Rather Than Keyword Claims

Indian commerce faculties already require a final-year project, dissertation or internship report from almost every student. That single assessment is the largest untapped source of SDG evidence on most campuses. Yet the typical file contains project titles that mention “sustainability,” “financial inclusion” or “green finance,” a list of student names, and little else. When a NAAC peer team, NBA visit or Times Higher Education Impact Rankings query asks for evidence that the institution contributed to specific SDG targets, the department produces the title list. The titles contain keywords. The projects themselves rarely contain a documented link between the work done and a measurable change, a usable data artefact, or a decision that an external party can inspect. This briefing gives heads of department, IQAC coordinators and project coordinators a practical protocol that converts the existing final-year project requirement into a verifiable SDG evidence file without adding a new course or a new bureaucratic layer.

The setting is Indian undergraduate and postgraduate commerce programmes after the National Education Policy’s emphasis on project-based learning, the growing use of SDG-tagged institutional claims in ranking exercises, and the practical reality that most faculty already supervise eight to twenty projects a year under teaching loads that leave little room for additional documentation. The protocol uses the documents the faculty already collects—proposal forms, mid-term reviews, final reports and viva sheets—and adds only the fields that turn a title into evidence. Evidence is not a count of projects that used an SDG keyword. It is a means of showing whether the student’s work produced a documented output that a stranger can inspect and that maps cleanly to one primary SDG target.

1. Why final-year projects are the highest-leverage SDG evidence channel

A final-year project has three properties that no awareness camp, guest lecture or MoU can match. First, it is already compulsory for every student, so the population is the full graduating cohort rather than a self-selected volunteer group. Second, it already generates a written artefact that must be submitted, assessed and retained for a minimum period under university rules. Third, the student spends between six and twelve weeks on the work, which is long enough for a modest but real investigation rather than a one-day activity. These properties make the project the highest-leverage channel for SDG evidence once the department decides what counts as a valid mapping.

Three failures follow a keyword approach. First, the denominator is the number of titles that contain an SDG-related word. A project titled “A Study of Green Banking Practices in Mumbai” is counted even if the student never collected primary data, never measured a practice, and never produced a usable finding. Second, the numerator has no outcome test. The project is finished when the viva is passed; nothing is recorded about whether the work generated a data table, a process map, a recommendation that an external organisation actually received, or a simple before-after observation. Third, the claim migrates. The departmental annual report lists “87 SDG-aligned projects.” The ranking form repeats the number. The peer team opens three sample reports and finds literature reviews that stop at the abstract of a newspaper article. The room goes quiet.

Working rule. No project is entered in the SDG evidence file until the proposal names one primary SDG target, the final report contains at least one artefact that a stranger can inspect without reading the entire narrative, and the supervisor’s sign-off confirms that the artefact was produced by the student. A keyword in the title is not a row.

Further reading on DrDPKlass: how campuses can map programmes to SDGs without SDG-washing and how faculties should map research papers to specific SDG targets without turning a keyword match into an impact claim. Project evidence sits next to both files. A title that never produces an inspectable artefact is the same error as an MoU that never produced shared activity or a research paper that was tagged only because the abstract contained the word “sustainable.”

2. Map each project to one primary target before the student starts

A single project can touch several targets. Split the mapping at the proposal stage, before the student begins data collection or literature work. Different targets create different evidence tests and different risks of over-claiming. The proposal form already exists; it needs only one additional mandatory field: “Primary SDG target (one number only) and one-sentence justification.”

DecisionWhat usually happensMinimum for a live project row
Primary targetAll projects tagged “SDG” or listed under several numbersExactly one primary target chosen with a one-sentence justification that a stranger can test against the planned artefact
Secondary targetsAdded to increase the total count of “SDG projects”Allowed in a private annex only; never added to the public headline number
Planned artefactLeft as “report will be submitted”Named on the proposal: a data table with source and date, a process map of an actual organisation, a simple before-after observation, or a recommendation letter that was sent
External boundaryIgnoredOne sentence on the setting (one firm, one branch, one campus unit, one cohort) so the claim cannot be read as institutional or national impact
Supervisor confirmationSigned only at the endSigned on the proposal so that the mapping is agreed before work begins

Write the primary target on the proposal before the student is allowed to begin. A project that studies the uptake of the Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana in one district can map primarily to Target 8.10 (strengthen the capacity of domestic financial institutions to encourage and expand access to banking, insurance and financial services for all). It does not automatically map to every inequalities or gender target. Say that. Means and ends are not the same row.

Do not. Map every project that mentions a social or environmental word, and do not map a literature review that never leaves the library. A literature review can be excellent academic training. It becomes an SDG mapping row only when the student produces an inspectable artefact that links the literature to a specific local observation or decision, and that artefact is documented.

Expiry belongs in the register. Projects completed in the reporting year are eligible for that year’s evidence file. Older projects may appear in a cumulative annex if a ranking form asks for a longer window, but they must be labelled by academic year. Backdating a new mapping onto last year’s projects to fatten this year’s table is the administrative version of channel stuffing.

3. The five-gate protocol that keeps evidence verifiable

The protocol has five gates. Each gate uses a document the faculty already collects. No new software is required. The only addition is three short fields and one mandatory artefact page.

Gate 1 – Proposal

Student names one primary target, one-sentence justification, planned artefact and external boundary. Supervisor signs. No work begins until this form is complete.

Gate 2 – Mid-term

Student submits a one-page progress note that shows the artefact is on track (sample table, draft process map, list of respondents). Supervisor notes any change in target or boundary.

Gate 3 – Final report

The report contains a dedicated one-page “Evidence Artefact” section that a stranger can read without the rest of the narrative. The artefact is dated and sourced.

Gate 4 – Viva sheet

The viva examiner confirms that the artefact exists and was produced by the student. A simple tick-box plus one sentence is sufficient.

Gate 5 – Departmental register

Only projects that pass Gates 1–4 enter the public SDG evidence file. The register records target, artefact type, boundary and academic year.

Take a familiar commerce example. A student proposes to study the effect of a bank’s digital onboarding process on first-time account holders in two branches of a public-sector bank. Primary target is 8.10. Planned artefact is a simple table of time-to-account-opening and number of documents required before and after the digital process, collected from branch records with permission. Boundary is “two branches of one bank, April–June 2026.” At mid-term the student shows a draft table with twenty observations. The final report contains the completed table with source note and date. The viva examiner ticks the artefact box. The project enters the evidence file as one row under Target 8.10. That is a mapping. It is not a claim that the university expanded financial inclusion in India.

If the only shared object is a title and a passed viva, you have an assessment record. Assessment records satisfy the university examination office. They are not the SDG evidence claim.
Cut-off test. Freeze the cohort population on the same date as the academic year ends. A late submission processed after the year closed belongs to next year. Artefacts dated after the cut-off do not move the table.

4. Supervisor load, student ownership and the anti-ghostwriting rule

Indian commerce faculty often supervise more projects than is pedagogically ideal. The protocol must not increase that load. It reduces it by making the evidence requirement clear at the start so that students do not produce long narrative reports that the supervisor must then reconstruct into evidence. The supervisor’s additional time is the five minutes required to sign the proposal and the two minutes required to tick the artefact box on the viva sheet. Everything else is already part of normal supervision.

Student ownership is the anti-ghostwriting rule. The artefact must be produced by the student. A table copied from a bank’s published annual report is not an artefact unless the student adds a calculation, a comparison or a local observation that is not already in the published document. A process map drawn by the student after visiting the organisation is an artefact. A recommendation letter written by the student and actually sent to the organisation (with a copy retained) is an artefact. The supervisor’s role is to confirm that the student did the work, not to supply the table.

Separation rule. The headline number of “SDG-aligned projects” may include only projects that have a confirmed artefact for the same student and the same academic year. Titles without artefacts stay in the ordinary project list. Mixing them produces a number that no peer team can reconstruct.

Group projects follow the same rule. Each student must be able to point to a part of the artefact that they produced. A single shared table is acceptable only if the viva examiner can ask any member of the group to explain the source and the calculation. Free-riding is already a known problem in group work; the artefact requirement makes it visible at the viva rather than invisible in a shared grade.

5. What the departmental evidence file must contain

The public evidence file is a simple spreadsheet or one-page-per-project register. It is not the full project reports. Full reports remain with the examination office or the department library as they always have. The evidence file contains only the fields needed for an external reviewer to see that the mapping is real.

  • Academic year and cohort size (total projects submitted).
  • Project identifier (student roll number or anonymised code) and primary SDG target.
  • One-sentence justification copied from the proposal.
  • Artefact type and a one-line description (for example, “table of account-opening time before/after digital process, 42 observations, two branches, June 2026”).
  • External boundary statement.
  • Supervisor confirmation date and viva examiner confirmation.
  • A note that the full report is available for inspection on request.

The file does not contain student names if the institution prefers anonymisation, nor does it contain the full narrative. It does contain enough for a peer-team member to ask to see three sample artefacts and to verify that they match the register. That verification takes twenty minutes. A title list takes the same twenty minutes and yields nothing verifiable.

Do not. Place the full project reports in the public evidence file. The public file is the register. Full reports are inspection material. Publishing full reports without student consent creates a data-protection problem that the protocol is not designed to solve.

6. The six project-washing errors that fail peer-team inspection

Peer teams and ranking agencies have seen the same six errors often enough that they now look for them first.

  1. Keyword inflation. Counting every title that contains “sustainable,” “inclusive,” “green” or “SDG.” The fix is the artefact gate.
  2. Multiple-target stacking. Listing one project under five targets to increase the total. The fix is the single primary-target rule.
  3. Literature-only claims. Treating a pure literature review as evidence of contribution to a target. The fix is the requirement for a local or applied artefact.
  4. Supervisor-supplied artefacts. The faculty member provides the data table and the student writes the narrative. The fix is the viva confirmation that the student produced the artefact.
  5. Boundary creep. A study of two branches is described as “the bank’s digital transformation” or “financial inclusion in the state.” The fix is the mandatory boundary sentence on the register.
  6. Year-end backdating. Adding last year’s projects to this year’s file after the ranking form opens. The fix is the academic-year cut-off.

Any one of these errors is enough for a careful reviewer to set the entire SDG project claim aside. The five-gate protocol prevents all six if it is applied before the academic year begins and is not relaxed in the final month.

7. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this checklist before the next academic year starts. It fits on one page and can be completed by the project coordinator in a single afternoon.

  • Proposal form updated with mandatory primary-target field, justification, planned artefact and boundary.
  • Mid-term progress note template created (one page).
  • Final-report template includes a dedicated “Evidence Artefact” page.
  • Viva sheet includes an artefact-confirmation tick-box.
  • Departmental register columns defined and a responsible person named.
  • Academic-year cut-off date agreed and written on the register cover.
  • Sample of three previous projects reviewed against the new gates to show faculty what will and will not count.
  • Students briefed in the first project-orientation session that a keyword is not sufficient.
Closing brief. The final-year project is already compulsory, already documented and already assessed. The only missing piece is the decision that an SDG claim requires an inspectable artefact mapped to one primary target before the student begins. Once that decision is made, the existing process produces the evidence file. No new course is required. No additional faculty post is required. The only requirement is that the department stops counting titles and starts counting artefacts that a stranger can verify in twenty minutes.

Indian commerce faculties do not lack projects. They lack a protocol that distinguishes a title from evidence. The five-gate protocol is that distinction. Apply it in the next academic year and the SDG evidence file will survive the peer team that opens the sample reports. Leave it as a keyword count and the same peer team will set the claim aside.
