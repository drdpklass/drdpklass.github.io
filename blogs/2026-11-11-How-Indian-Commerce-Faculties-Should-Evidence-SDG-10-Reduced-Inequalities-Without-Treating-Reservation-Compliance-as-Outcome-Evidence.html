How Indian Commerce Faculties Should Evidence SDG 10 Reduced Inequalities Without Treating Reservation Compliance as Outcome Evidence

Indian commerce faculties routinely list statutory reservation percentages, fee-waiver counts and the existence of an equal-opportunity cell when asked to demonstrate progress on SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities). Those items satisfy legal compliance and can support an activity register. They do not, by themselves, evidence reduced inequalities in outcomes. SDG 10 targets concern both the reduction of income and opportunity gaps and the progressive achievement of greater equality. A filled reservation roster shows that seats were allocated according to law. It does not show whether the students who entered through those seats completed the programme at comparable rates, participated in high-value classroom experiences, or secured placement outcomes that narrow rather than widen prior gaps. This briefing gives department heads, IQAC coordinators and placement cells a practical protocol for building an SDG 10 evidence file that a peer team, ranking agency or external reviewer can inspect without mistaking compliance for outcome.

The setting is Indian higher education after NAAC’s emphasis on equity and inclusion metrics, NIRF’s outreach and inclusivity indicators, and the growing use of Times Higher Education Impact Rankings and similar exercises that invite SDG-tagged institutional claims. The same discipline applies to an affiliating university reviewing a college’s self-study and to a single faculty member preparing an equity statement for promotion or a board of studies. Evidence is not public relations. It is a means of showing whether the programme changes the distribution of outcomes for students who arrive with systematically different prior resources.

1. Why reservation compliance is not SDG 10 evidence

Target 10.2 under the SDGs calls for empowering and promoting the social, economic and political inclusion of all, irrespective of age, sex, disability, race, ethnicity, origin, religion or economic or other status. Target 10.3 concerns equal opportunity and reduced inequalities of outcome, including the elimination of discriminatory laws, policies and practices. Indian reservation policy is a statutory instrument designed to correct historical exclusion. Compliance with that instrument is necessary. It is not the same as evidence that inequalities of outcome have been reduced inside the commerce programme.

Three failures follow a pure compliance approach. First, the denominator is the sanctioned intake, not the distribution of prior academic or economic resources. A department that fills every reserved seat still has no measure of whether those students enter with systematically lower foundation scores, higher first-generation status, or greater geographic and language distance from the medium of instruction. Second, the numerator has no outcome test. Seats filled do not reveal completion rates, grade distributions, participation in internships or consulting projects, or placement packages once prior attainment is held constant. Third, the claim migrates. The departmental newsletter lists “full compliance with reservation norms.” The ranking form repeats the percentage. The peer team opens the placement report and finds that median packages for the reserved category lag the general category by a margin larger than the gap in entry-level scores. The room goes quiet.

Working rule. No SDG 10 claim leaves the department until the file separates statutory compliance (activity) from at least one outcome measure that a stranger can inspect: completion rates by category, grade distributions conditional on entry scores, or placement outcomes with a simple control for prior attainment. Compliance without that measure is a legal report, not an inequalities evidence file.

Inequalities evidence sits next to both files. A filled roster that never tracks subsequent outcomes is the same error as an MoU that never produced shared activity or a leadership count that never tested decision rights.

2. Map to specific targets before counting activities

A single equity initiative can touch several targets. Split the mapping before you write the narrative. Different targets create different evidence tests and different risks of over-claiming.

DecisionWhat usually happens on an Indian campusMinimum for a live SDG 10 row
Primary targetAll equity activity tagged to “SDG 10”One primary target (typically 10.2 or 10.3) chosen with a one-sentence justification that a stranger can test against the activity and the outcome measure
Secondary targetsListed in a long string to increase the total countAllowed in an annex only; never added to the headline number of “SDG 10 initiatives”
Activity descriptionLeft as “reservation followed” or “scholarship given”Stated in the register in plain language that names the instrument, the population and the reporting year
Outcome measureOmitted or replaced by activity countNamed (completion rate differential, grade gap conditional on entry score, placement package gap with control) so the reader can see whether the design can speak to reduced inequality of outcome
External validity or boundaryIgnoredOne sentence on the setting (one programme, one campus, one cohort) so the claim cannot be read as institutional transformation

Write the primary target on the register before you write the SDG code into any public document. A fee-waiver scheme that increases enrolment of students from the lowest income quintile can map primarily to Target 10.2; it does not automatically map to every inequalities-related target. Say that. Means and ends are not the same row.

Do not. Map every statutory compliance item, and do not map a one-day awareness camp that merely distributes leaflets. Compliance is not a research or teaching contribution to a target. An awareness camp can be useful outreach. It becomes an SDG 10 mapping row only when the camp is linked to a subsequent measurable change in participation or outcome, and that change is documented for the same cohort.

Expiry and dormancy belong in the register. Indian faculties sometimes list reservation percentages from five years ago because the law has not changed. The live file should carry a reporting-year cut-off. Activities and outcome measures from the reporting year are eligible. Older figures may appear in a cumulative annex if the ranking form asks for a longer window, but they must be labelled by year. Backdating a new scholarship to fatten this year’s table is the administrative version of channel stuffing.

3. Evidence in admissions that goes beyond the roster

Admissions evidence has four rungs. The instrument is the object: reservation category, income-based scholarship, disability support, or geographic outreach. The target is the primary SDG target chosen with a justification. The evidence is the specific intake or retention figure that supports the link. The claim is the institutional sentence that will appear in the self-study. Most campus files stop at the instrument and the filled percentage and call it a claim. The honest file labels the rung.

Instrument

Named policy or scheme, eligible population, and the reporting year. A circular without an executed list is not yet a row.

Target

One primary target number and a one-sentence reason that quotes or paraphrases the target language and the intended mechanism.

Evidence

The specific figure a stranger can check: seats filled versus sanctioned, or better, application-to-enrolment conversion by category and prior-score band. “Reservation was followed” is not evidence.

Claim

The institutional sentence limited to what the instrument actually did. “In 2025–26 the programme filled 100 per cent of reserved seats and recorded application rates from the lowest income quintile that were X per cent of the general category rate.” Do not write “the university reduced inequality.”

Take a familiar commerce example. A department operates a state-mandated reservation plus an additional income-based fee waiver for students whose family income falls below a published threshold. Activity is the executed roster and the waiver list. Output is the published intake table with category-wise numbers. The primary target might be 10.2. Evidence is the application rate and the enrolment rate for the income-waiver group relative to the overall pool, plus the share of first-generation students in that group. The claim sentence is: “In 2025–26 the department admitted a cohort in which the lowest income quintile formed Y per cent of intake (versus Z per cent of the state 18–22 population) and recorded a first-generation share of W per cent.” That is a mapping. It is not a claim that the university closed the income gap in higher education.

If the only shared object is a filled roster and a compliance certificate, you have a legal report. Legal reports can satisfy regulators. They are not the inequalities claim.
Cut-off test. Freeze the cohort population on the same date as the annual report. A late admission processed after the year closed belongs to next year. Waiver lists dated after the cut-off do not move the table.

4. Classroom participation and learning outcomes

Classroom evidence is harder and more valuable. Indian commerce programmes often run large lectures. Participation is uneven. Students who entered with lower foundation scores or who are first-generation may sit at the back, avoid spoken contributions, and receive less formative feedback. A department that stops at the admissions roster has no view of whether those gaps narrow or widen inside the programme.

Useful measures do not require sophisticated econometrics. They require consistency. Record, for each major assessment, the grade distribution by entry-score band and by reservation or income category. Record attendance at optional high-value activities (case competitions, consulting projects, research assistantships) by the same groups. Record the share of students who receive at least one piece of written formative feedback that names a specific improvement. These figures can be assembled from existing mark sheets and activity registers. They do not require new surveys if the registers are already kept.

Separation rule. The headline number of “equity initiatives” may include only activities that have at least one linked outcome measure for the same cohort. Statutory compliance stays in its own column. Mixing them produces a number that no peer team can reconstruct.

Curriculum adjustment is a common migration path. A department adds a bridge module for students below a foundation-score threshold. The module can be excellent teaching. It becomes evidence of reduced inequality of outcome only when the board of studies minutes name the rationale, the syllabus is filed, and subsequent grade or completion differentials for the same entry-score band are reported. The equity row describes the module and the differential; it does not claim that the university solved the foundation problem.

5. Placement and early-career outcomes that survive scrutiny

Placement reports are the most common place where inequalities claims collapse. Indian commerce faculties publish median and average packages. They rarely publish the same figures conditional on entry scores or by reservation or income category. A peer team that asks for the conditional gap receives either silence or a defensive statement that “all students are treated equally.” Equal treatment is not the same as reduced inequality of outcome when prior resources differ systematically.

A practical protocol uses data the placement cell already holds. For each graduating cohort, compute the median package by entry-score quartile and by reservation or income category. Report the gap in median packages between the top and bottom entry-score quartiles, and the same gap inside the reserved category. Report the share of students from the lowest income quintile who received at least one offer above the programme median. These calculations can be done in a spreadsheet from the existing placement register and the admissions register. They do not require personal identifiers to leave the cell if the tables are aggregated.

Do not. Publish an unadjusted median package and claim it demonstrates reduced inequality. Unadjusted figures can hide the fact that higher packages are concentrated among students who entered with higher foundation scores. The honest table shows both the unadjusted and the conditional gaps. If the conditional gap is large, the file states the gap and the actions the department will take next year. Silence is not evidence.

Early-career follow-up is stronger still and rarer. A simple six-month or one-year check with alumni who consented can record whether students from the lowest income quintile remain in formal employment at rates comparable to their peers. Even a modest response rate, reported with the response rate, is better than no figure. The claim remains limited: “Among the Z alumni who responded, the formal-employment rate for the lowest income quintile was X per cent versus Y per cent for the rest of the cohort.” That is an outcome measure. It is not a claim that the university eliminated labour-market inequality.

6. Avoid the five inequalities-washing errors

Five patterns convert a compliance file into an inequalities-washing claim.

  1. Roster as outcome. Treating filled reserved seats as proof that inequalities have been reduced. The roster is an activity. Outcome requires a subsequent measure.
  2. Activity inflation. Counting every scholarship, every awareness camp and every compliance certificate as a separate “SDG 10 initiative.” The headline number becomes meaningless.
  3. Unconditional placement figures. Publishing median packages without conditioning on entry scores or prior resources. The figure can move in the wrong direction while the claim stays positive.
  4. Missing cohort link. Reporting an initiative for one year and an outcome for a different cohort. The reader cannot test whether the instrument and the measure belong together.
  5. Universal language. Writing “the university has reduced inequalities” when the evidence covers one programme on one campus for one cohort. Boundary language is required.

Each error is avoidable with the register structure already described. The cost of avoidance is an extra column and an extra sentence. The cost of the error is a peer-team query that the department cannot answer from the file it submitted.

7. Field checklist and closing brief

Before any SDG 10 sentence leaves the department, run this checklist.

  • Primary target named (usually 10.2 or 10.3) with a one-sentence justification.
  • Instrument or activity described in plain language with population and year.
  • At least one outcome measure attached that a stranger can inspect (completion differential, conditional grade gap, or conditional placement gap).
  • Statutory compliance figures kept in a separate column from outcome measures.
  • Cohort link explicit: the outcome measure refers to the same students who received the instrument.
  • Boundary sentence present: one programme, one campus, one reporting year.
  • Cut-off date aligned with the annual report; no backdating.
  • No universal claim (“the university reduced inequality”) unless the evidence actually supports it.
Closing rule. If the file cannot survive a peer-team request for the conditional placement gap or the completion rate by entry-score band, it is not yet an SDG 10 evidence file. Add the measure or narrow the claim. Do not publish the stronger sentence.

Indian commerce faculties already hold most of the data required. Admissions registers, mark sheets and placement lists exist. The missing step is the deliberate construction of a small number of conditional outcome measures and the discipline to keep those measures separate from statutory compliance. That step turns a legal report into an evidence file a stranger can test. It also gives the department a clearer view of where inequalities of outcome persist and where the next teaching or support intervention should be aimed.

The work is ordinary. It is also the only work that survives contact with a careful reader.
