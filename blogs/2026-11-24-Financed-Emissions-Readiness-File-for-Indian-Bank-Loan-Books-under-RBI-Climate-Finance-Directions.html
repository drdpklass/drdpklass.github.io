On 28 November 2025 the Reserve Bank of India issued the Reserve Bank of India (Commercial Banks – Climate Finance and Management of Climate Change Risks) Directions, 2025 (and parallel instruments for Small Finance Banks and NBFCs). The Directions require boards to approve a financing framework for green activities, integrate climate-change risk considerations into existing risk-management structures, and take steps that protect depositors while reducing greenwashing risk. They do not yet mandate a full financed-emissions disclosure table. That distinction matters. Many Indian banks have begun to treat the Directions as a green-deposit compliance exercise and have left the loan-book climate-risk integration for a later cycle. The practical consequence is that when rating agencies, bilateral lenders, or a future RBI disclosure framework ask for an estimate of financed emissions, the bank discovers it has neither the exposure map nor the data lineage to produce one without inventing numbers. This briefing supplies a readiness protocol that produces a one-page matrix a board or risk committee can review in ten minutes. The method uses data the bank already holds—sectoral loan-book exposures, internal risk grades, and the BRSR filings of its large corporate borrowers—and does not require a proprietary climate model. The goal is simple: either the bank can demonstrate that it has a documented, non-invented pathway from its current loan book to a financed-emissions estimate, or the board sees the precise data gap that must be closed before any public claim is made.

What the RBI Climate Finance Directions actually require of boards

The Directions are structured around green deposits, a board-approved financing framework, third-party verification, impact assessment, and the integration of climate-change risks into the bank’s existing risk-management framework. For the purpose of financed-emissions readiness, three provisions are decisive. First, the board must approve a financing framework that identifies eligible green activities, sets out the process for project evaluation and selection, and addresses monitoring and validation of information supplied by borrowers. Second, the bank is expected to carry out a comprehensive assessment of climate-change risks and to integrate those considerations into its extant risk-management structures. Third, the framework must operate in a manner that overcomes greenwashing challenges. None of these provisions currently requires the publication of a Scope-3 financed-emissions number. They do, however, require the board to be able to answer a straightforward question: “If we were asked tomorrow to estimate the carbon intensity of our corporate loan book, what data would we use, what assumptions would we document, and which exposures would we flag as data-deficient?”

Indian banks that treat the Directions solely as a green-deposit product rule miss this point. A green-deposit policy can be written and approved without any change to the way the credit risk function looks at the existing loan book. The readiness file described here is the bridge. It converts the general obligation to integrate climate risk into a concrete, auditable artefact that sits inside the risk committee’s ordinary agenda. Further reading on DrDPKlass: earlier ESG briefings on constructing a physical climate-risk heatmap and on building a Scope-3 boundary without inventing supplier emissions supply the physical-risk and corporate Scope-3 context for the financed-emissions protocol that follows.

Callout. A board minute that records “the financing framework was approved” is not evidence that climate risk has been integrated into the loan book. The minute that records the residual data gap in the financed-emissions readiness file is.

Why financed emissions are the binding constraint for Indian loan books

For most Indian commercial banks the largest climate-related exposure is not the bank’s own Scope 1 and 2 emissions; it is the financed emissions associated with its corporate and project-finance portfolios. Thermal-power, steel, cement, refining, and basic-chemicals exposures dominate absolute financed emissions even when they represent a modest share of the total book. MSME and retail portfolios are material for physical risk and for transition risk in certain sectors, but they contribute far less to the absolute financed-emissions number that rating agencies and future disclosure regimes are likely to examine first. The binding constraint is therefore not the absence of a climate model; it is the absence of a documented, sector-level exposure map that can be updated quarterly from the same systems that already feed the credit-risk MIS.

Two features of the Indian market make this constraint tighter than it appears. First, a substantial fraction of the large-corporate book consists of listed companies that already file BRSR reports. Those reports contain Scope 1 and 2 emissions and, increasingly, limited Scope 3 information. The data exist; they are simply not systematically extracted into the bank’s credit file. Second, the remaining exposures—unlisted corporates, project SPVs, and smaller NBFCs on-lent through—have no public emissions data. Any readiness file that pretends those exposures can be estimated with the same confidence as the BRSR-covered book will fail an auditor or rating-agency challenge. The protocol therefore separates the book into a “BRSR-covered” tier and a “data-deficient” tier and treats the second tier as a residual that must be disclosed as such rather than filled with industry averages that the bank cannot defend.

Constructing the sectoral exposure map without inventing emissions factors

The first artefact is a sectoral exposure map that the credit-risk MIS can produce without external data. Recommended fields for each major sector (thermal power, steel, cement, refining, chemicals, commercial real estate, logistics, and a residual “other”) are:

  • Gross exposure and outstanding amount as of the reporting date.
  • Number of borrowers and share of the corporate book.
  • Weighted-average internal risk grade and share of the exposure that is currently watch-list or non-performing.
  • Share of the exposure that is project finance versus corporate finance.
  • Share of the exposure for which the bank already holds a recent BRSR or equivalent emissions disclosure from the borrower.

The map does not yet contain an emissions number. Its purpose is to show the board which slices of the book would drive any future financed-emissions estimate and which slices lack the underlying data. In practice, Indian banks that have run this exercise discover that 60–75 percent of their absolute high-carbon exposure sits in fewer than 50 named counterparties, most of which are listed and therefore already produce BRSR data. The remaining exposure is granular and data-deficient. Once this fact is visible, the data-collection priority becomes obvious: extract and store the BRSR emissions figures for the large counterparties, and document a residual methodology for the rest that does not claim precision it does not possess.

India-specific note. Many mid-market and project-finance exposures are held through consortiums. The readiness file should record whether the bank is the lead or a participant, because only the lead typically receives the detailed project-level emissions data.

Linking borrower BRSR data to the bank’s financed-emissions estimate

For the BRSR-covered tier the protocol is deliberately conservative. The bank extracts, for each large counterparty, the most recent reported Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions and the reporting boundary (standalone or consolidated). It then applies a simple attribution factor equal to the bank’s outstanding exposure divided by the borrower’s total debt or enterprise value, whichever is more reliably available. The resulting attributed emissions are summed by sector. Two rules prevent invention:

  1. If the borrower has not reported emissions, or has reported them only on a boundary that excludes the financed asset, the exposure remains in the data-deficient tier.
  2. No industry-average emissions factor is applied to a named counterparty that has its own BRSR filing. Averages are used only for the residual data-deficient tier, and only after the board has been shown the sensitivity of the residual number to the choice of factor.

The output is not a single “financed emissions” figure. It is a two-column table: “BRSR-attributed emissions (tCO2e)” and “data-deficient exposure (₹ crore)”. The second column is the quantity the board must decide how to treat—whether to accept a residual estimate with wide confidence bounds, to commission primary data collection for the largest data-deficient names, or to disclose the residual explicitly in any future external communication. Indian banks that have tried to collapse both columns into one number have later been forced to restate when a large borrower revised its BRSR comparative figures, a pattern already visible in recent analyses of Nifty-100 BRSR revisions.

The same extraction process creates an automatic link to the bank’s existing ESG data-lineage practices. Once the BRSR figures and the attribution calculation sit in a controlled repository, the bank can demonstrate to an internal auditor or a rating agency that the financed-emissions pathway is reproducible. Earlier DrDPKlass work on building an ESG data-lineage file supplies the control framework that should govern this repository.

Governance ownership and the one-page board matrix

Because the readiness file will influence both internal risk appetite and any future external claim, ownership must be unambiguous. Recommended practice:

  • Credit risk or the chief risk officer’s office owns the sectoral exposure map and the attribution calculation; the sustainability or ESG team supplies sector definitions and helps interpret borrower BRSR footnotes but does not own the exposure numbers.
  • The methodology note (attribution rule, treatment of consortiums, residual methodology) is a two-page document that is updated only when a material change occurs.
  • The input extracts (loan-book MIS, BRSR data pulls) and the final matrix are stored in the same repository used for other risk-committee papers so that internal audit can sample them.
  • The risk committee receives the one-page matrix at least annually, or whenever a material change in the high-carbon book occurs.

The matrix itself contains four rows: BRSR-covered high-carbon exposure, data-deficient high-carbon exposure, residual attributed emissions (with the residual methodology stated), and the list of the ten largest data-deficient counterparties. A short “actions required” row records the decisions the board or risk committee must take—whether to request primary data from specific names, whether to adjust internal risk appetite for data-deficient sectors, or whether to defer any public financed-emissions claim until the residual falls below an internal threshold. A board minute that records these decisions is the evidence that climate-risk integration has occurred. A minute that merely “notes the Climate Finance Directions” is not.

Common failure modes in Indian bank ESG packs

Four patterns appear repeatedly. First, the bank produces a green-deposit policy and treats the Directions as satisfied, leaving the loan-book climate-risk work for a later date. Second, an external consultant supplies a single financed-emissions number that applies industry averages to the entire book, including named counterparties that already file BRSR reports; the number cannot be reconciled to the bank’s own credit files. Third, consortium and project-finance exposures are omitted or double-counted because the MIS does not distinguish lead and participant roles. Fourth, the pack shows only the base-case attributed number and does not display the data-deficient residual, so the board never sees the uncertainty. A quieter fifth failure is the absence of a feedback loop: after a large borrower revises its BRSR comparative figures, the readiness file is not re-run, so the board continues to rely on an outdated attribution.

Each of these failures is avoidable with the protocol above. The additional work is modest—most of the exposure data already exist inside the credit-risk MIS, and the BRSR filings of listed borrowers are public—and the reduction in later surprise is large. Banks that complete the readiness file before any external disclosure mandate arrives will be able to respond to rating-agency or supervisory queries with a documented residual rather than with a newly invented number.

Field checklist for the next risk-committee or board pack

  • Sectoral exposure map extracted from the credit-risk MIS, showing high-carbon sectors, share of BRSR coverage, and consortium versus bilateral status.
  • BRSR-attributed emissions calculated only for counterparties with usable reported figures; no industry average applied to named BRSR filers.
  • Data-deficient residual quantified in ₹ crore and listed by the ten largest names; residual methodology documented and sensitivity shown.
  • Ownership assigned: credit risk owns the numbers; ESG team owns sector definitions and BRSR interpretation.
  • One-page matrix prepared with actions-required row; methodology note and input extracts stored in the risk-committee repository.
  • Board or risk-committee minute records the residual data gap and the specific decisions taken (primary data request, risk-appetite adjustment, or deferral of public claim).
  • Feedback loop defined: readiness file re-run if a material borrower revises its BRSR comparative figures or if high-carbon exposure changes by more than an internal threshold.

The RBI Climate Finance Directions have moved climate-risk integration from optional practice to a board-level expectation. They have not yet required a public financed-emissions table. The banks that use the intervening period to build a documented, non-invented readiness file will be able to answer the next supervisory or rating-agency question with evidence rather than with a newly constructed number. Those that wait will discover, under time pressure, that the data they need were already in their own credit files and in their borrowers’ BRSR reports.
