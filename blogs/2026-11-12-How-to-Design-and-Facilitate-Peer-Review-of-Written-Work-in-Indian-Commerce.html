How to Design and Facilitate Peer Review of Written Work in Indian Commerce So Students Revise Before Faculty Ever Sees the Draft

Most Indian commerce courses still treat the first written submission as the version the faculty must mark. Students produce a draft under time pressure, hand it in, and receive comments weeks later. By then the next assignment has already been set. The comments, however carefully written, arrive too late to change the habits that produced the first draft. First-generation students, who have rarely seen a worked example of the expected structure, leave the process with a mark but without a revised piece of work that demonstrates improvement.

A structured peer-review protocol moves the first revision cycle entirely into the classroom and between students. Each student produces a short written piece, receives structured comments from two peers using a fixed checklist, revises the piece in the same or the next class, and only then submits the revised version for faculty assessment. The faculty never sees the first draft. The design requires one class period for writing and review, a short calibration exercise, and a binary check that the revision actually used the peer comments. It fits inside an ordinary continuous-assessment scheme and produces evidence that students practised the skill of giving and using feedback.

This note is written for faculty who teach ordinary sixty- or eighty-seat commerce rooms with no teaching assistant. The protocol uses only a one-page checklist, pairs or triads formed in the room, and five minutes of faculty time between classes to confirm that revisions occurred. It does not require online platforms, though it can run on paper in rooms with limited connectivity.

1. Why faculty marking of first drafts multiplies load without improving the second draft

Faculty marking of first drafts fails as a learning device for three reasons visible in Indian commerce classrooms. First, the lag between submission and return is long. A draft submitted in week four is often returned in week seven. Students have already moved to the next topic and have little incentive to revise the earlier piece. Second, the volume is high. Marking sixty first drafts of a two-page memo consumes an entire weekend. The comments that result are often general because the faculty has no remaining time for line-level notes. Third, first-generation students rarely possess a mental model of the expected structure. They produce a first draft that lists facts without a clear claim, and the faculty comment “improve structure” does not show them what the improved structure looks like.

Callout: A first draft marked by faculty after a multi-week lag is not evidence of learning. It is evidence that the cost of producing a revised version earlier was higher than the cost of accepting a single mark. A peer-review cycle lowers that cost to the act of reading one peer’s draft against a fixed checklist and rewriting one paragraph before the next class ends.

The alternative is to treat the first draft as an intermediate product that students must revise before any faculty mark is assigned. The revision is guided by peer comments that follow a checklist the faculty has already calibrated with the class. The faculty marks only the revised version. The load falls because the faculty never reads the first draft, and the learning rises because every student produces at least one revised piece that incorporates external comments.

2. Designing a structured peer-review protocol that first-generation students can complete in one class period

An effective peer-review protocol for Indian commerce classrooms has four properties: short enough for one class period, specific enough that a first-generation student can decide whether a criterion is met, binary enough to complete without lengthy writing, and identical for every pair so the pattern of problems is visible to the faculty.

The written task itself is limited to 250–350 words. Typical tasks include a one-page memo that recommends a treatment of a provision versus a reserve, a short analysis of a published Indian company note, or a response to a regulatory extract. Students write the piece in the first twenty minutes of class or bring a completed draft from the previous evening. The checklist contains five binary items: (1) the piece opens with a single claim rather than a definition; (2) the claim is supported by one specific number or extract from the source; (3) the piece names one alternative that was rejected; (4) the conclusion restates the claim in different words; (5) every sentence can be understood without the reader already knowing the answer. Students circle yes or no for each item and write one sentence of evidence only for the first “no.”

Pairs are formed by proximity or by a simple numbering system. Each student reads the partner’s draft against the checklist for twelve minutes, then returns the marked checklist. The writer has fifteen minutes to revise one paragraph or the opening claim using the comments. The revised piece and the marked checklist are collected at the end of the period. The faculty does not read the first draft. Only the revised version is assessed later.

3. A fifteen-minute calibration exercise that stops polite comments from becoming the default

Without calibration, peer comments in Indian classrooms tend toward politeness. Students write “good effort” or “please check grammar” because they have never seen a concrete example of a useful comment. A fifteen-minute calibration exercise at the start of the cycle removes that default.

The faculty projects or distributes one anonymous past draft of roughly the same length and topic. The class works through the five checklist items together. For each item the faculty asks three pairs to report their yes/no and the single sentence of evidence. The faculty records the modal answer on the board and states the correct classification. The exercise ends when the class has agreed on the classification for all five items. The anonymous draft is then set aside. Students begin the real task knowing what a “no” looks like and what evidence is required.

Practical rule: If more than one-third of the pairs still mark a clear structural failure as “yes” during calibration, do not proceed to the real task. Repeat the calibration with a second anonymous example the same day. Coverage of the next topic is less important than evidence that students can apply the checklist.

The calibration draft is chosen from the previous year’s submissions or from a deliberately imperfect example the faculty has prepared. It is never a current student’s work. After the exercise, students understand that a useful comment identifies a specific missing element rather than offering general encouragement.

4. A three-meeting cycle that forces revision before any faculty mark is assigned

The peer-review protocol is useful only if it changes what students submit for assessment. A three-meeting cycle makes the change routine.

Meeting one. Students write the short piece in class or bring a completed draft. The faculty runs the fifteen-minute calibration. Pairs complete the checklist and return it. Writers revise one paragraph before leaving the room. The revised pieces and checklists are collected. No marks are assigned.

Meeting two. The faculty returns the revised pieces with a binary stamp: “revision used the peer comment” or “revision did not address the marked item.” Students who received the second stamp have ten minutes to revise again using the original checklist. The faculty collects only the second revisions from those students. The remaining students begin the next planned activity. The binary stamp is the only faculty mark recorded from the process so far.

Meeting three. Students submit the final revised version for ordinary continuous-assessment marking. The faculty marks the final version against the same five criteria used in the checklist. The peer checklists and the binary stamps remain in the evidence folder but are not re-marked. The cycle fits inside three ordinary class meetings. No extra hour is added to the timetable.

Students who miss meeting one may complete the writing and peer review with a partner outside class and submit both the first and revised versions by the start of meeting two. The rule is stated on the board at the beginning of the cycle so that absence does not become a negotiation.

5. The three writing problems that dominate Indian commerce peer reviews

After several cycles the same three problems appear on the “no” side of the checklist, regardless of the specific topic. Naming them in advance lets the faculty write prompts and calibration examples that surface them quickly.

The first is the definition opening. Students who have been asked to recommend a treatment still begin with “A provision is defined as..." The checklist item that surfaces it is the first: does the piece open with a single claim rather than a definition? A “no” requires the writer to rewrite the opening sentence so that it states the recommendation. The revision is completed before the student leaves the room.

The second is the unsupported claim. Students write that a company should treat an item as a provision without citing the single number or note that would change. The checklist item that surfaces it asks whether the claim is supported by one specific number or extract. A “no” requires the writer to insert the missing number or sentence from the source document. The source is available in the room or was distributed the previous class.

The third is the missing alternative. Students state a recommendation without naming what they rejected. The checklist item that surfaces it asks whether the piece names one alternative that was rejected. A “no” requires the writer to add one sentence that names the rejected treatment and the reason it was set aside. None of these problems requires a separate remedial class. They appear on the peer checklists and are corrected in the revision window of the same meeting.

6. Evidence for continuous assessment and NAAC without individual faculty comments on every draft

Indian continuous-assessment and NAAC documentation both ask for evidence that students practised a skill and that the faculty responded to what the students produced. The peer-review cycle produces three items that can be filed in one folder: the peer checklists with the binary marks, the binary stamps that confirm revision occurred, and a one-page summary that records how many drafts failed each checklist item after the first meeting. These items sit inside the continuous-assessment band already approved for the paper.

When an external examiner or an IQAC member asks how written communication was developed, the folder plus the one-sentence learning target written on the first page is the answer. No additional register or photographic evidence is required. The faculty marks only the final revised version. Individual line-by-line comments on first drafts are never generated. The peer comments and the binary revision check constitute the formative evidence.

For the marks register, allocate a small continuous-assessment weight to the completion of the peer review and the revision. The final version receives the ordinary marks for the assignment. Students who produce a first draft that fails three or more checklist items are not penalised beyond the requirement to revise; they simply complete the revision before the final submission is accepted. The requirement is stated at the start of the cycle.

7. Protecting the teaching load across multiple sections and large cohorts

Faculty who teach the same paper to two or three sections can reuse the identical checklist and the identical calibration draft. The modal failure from the first section often predicts the modal failure from the second; when it does, the faculty can prepare a one-sentence board note in advance. When the pattern differs, the faculty simply records the new modal item. Preparation time does not double.

The fifteen-minute calibration and the revision window occur inside the ordinary class period. No new hour is created on the timetable and no hour is added to the weekend. The faculty glances at the collected checklists only long enough to stamp “revision used” or “revision not used.” The stamp takes less than five minutes per section. The evidence folder is updated once, at the end of the third meeting. Faculty who already run office hours can use the first five minutes of the existing hour for students who still cannot produce a claim sentence. Faculty who do not run formal office hours can stand at the door for the same five minutes after the third meeting. The students who need the additional correction are few because the peer review has already forced most of them to rewrite at least one paragraph.

In rooms larger than eighty seats the same protocol runs with triads rather than pairs. Each student reviews one draft and is reviewed by one peer. The time allocation remains unchanged. The only adjustment is that the faculty collects one checklist per student rather than per pair. The binary stamp is applied in the same five-minute window between classes.

8. Closing field checklist for the peer-review cycle

Before the cycle begins:

  • Write the one-sentence target for the three meetings: every student will revise at least one paragraph using a peer checklist before the faculty marks the work.
  • Prepare the five-item binary checklist and one anonymous calibration draft.
  • Decide the binary revision stamp and write the rule on the board for meeting one.
  • Identify the class periods that will contain the writing, review, and revision windows.

During the three meetings:

  • Meeting one: run calibration; students write or bring drafts; pairs complete checklists; writers revise one paragraph before leaving.
  • Meeting two: return pieces with binary stamps; students who did not revise complete a second revision; others proceed to the next activity.
  • Meeting three: collect final revised versions for ordinary marking; file the checklists and stamps.

After the cycle:

  • File the checklists, the binary stamps, and the one-page summary of modal failures.
  • Invite the few students who still cannot produce a claim sentence to the ordinary five-minute slot after class.
  • Reuse the identical checklist and calibration draft next year with only the company name or extract changed.

The peer-review cycle does not add an hour to the timetable and does not require a teaching assistant. It converts the ordinary class period into a sequence in which every student produces a revised piece of written work before any faculty mark is assigned. When the final assessment arrives, the first draft has already been forced to compete with external comments under low stakes. The students who still need further correction are few, and the faculty already knows who they are from the binary stamps.
