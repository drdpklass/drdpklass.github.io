How Indian Finance Scholars Should Write a Pre-Submission Journal-Fit Memo That Stops the Wrong Desk Reject

Most desk rejects that Indian finance scholars receive are not rejections of the idea. They are rejections of the match. An editor who has spent eight minutes on the introduction and the identification section decides that the paper belongs in a different conversation, a different data environment, or a different methodological tradition. The manuscript is returned with a short note that the contribution is incremental or that the design does not speak to the journal’s audience. The author then spends another three months revising for a second journal that has the same mismatch. A short pre-submission journal-fit memo, written before the cover letter and before the final polish, stops most of these cycles. This brief shows how to write that memo so that the decision to submit is evidence-based rather than hopeful, and so that the cover letter and introduction can be written to the actual audience rather than to a generic finance editor.

Why the Wrong Journal Produces the Fastest Desk Reject

Desk editors at the journals that Indian finance researchers typically target—Journal of Corporate Finance, Journal of Banking and Finance, Pacific-Basin Finance Journal, Emerging Markets Review, Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money, and the stronger Indian outlets—screen for fit before they screen for technical quality. Fit is not the same as quality. A clean difference-in-differences design on Indian bank lending can be excellent work and still be the wrong paper for a journal whose recent issues are dominated by high-frequency market-microstructure studies or by theoretical models of information asymmetry. The editor’s note will not say “wrong journal.” It will say “the contribution is not sufficiently novel for our readership” or “the institutional setting limits the general interest of the findings.” Both sentences are fit statements.

The cost of the mismatch is the opportunity cost of the revision cycle. When the paper is later sent to a better-matched journal, the introduction and literature review have usually been written for the first journal’s audience. The second editor sees language that does not quite land. Early-career scholars feel the cost most acutely because each desk reject delays the API score or the next promotion cycle by six to nine months.

Indian data environments make the fit problem sharper. Many journals that publish emerging-market papers still prefer designs that speak to a mechanism that is not purely India-specific. A paper that exploits the staggered implementation of a SEBI LODR amendment can be a strong fit for Emerging Markets Review or the Journal of Corporate Finance special issues on regulation; the same paper can be a poor fit for a journal that publishes almost exclusively on U.S. or European samples and that treats single-country institutional detail as a limitation rather than a feature. The memo forces the author to decide, before submission, which of these audiences is the real one.

Callout. If you cannot name three recent papers in the target journal that share your paper’s economic question or identification problem, the memo will almost certainly recommend a different outlet.

What a Journal-Fit Memo Actually Contains

The memo is not a second cover letter and it is not a contribution paragraph. It is an internal document, usually one page, that answers five questions for the co-author team before any external submission is made. The questions are:

  • What conversation is this journal currently running, as evidenced by its last twelve to eighteen months of published and forthcoming papers?
  • Where does our paper enter that conversation, and which of the journal’s recent papers would a reviewer be expected to cite when evaluating us?
  • What is the single most likely reason an editor at this journal would desk-reject the paper on fit grounds?
  • What sentence in the introduction must be true for the fit claim to hold, and can we defend that sentence with evidence already in the paper?
  • If the answer to the previous questions is marginal, which alternative journal clears the same bar more cleanly?

The memo is written after the first complete draft and after the identification and contribution paragraphs have stabilised, but before the cover letter is drafted and before the paper is formatted to the journal’s style. Writing it earlier is useful for planning; writing it later is too late because the sunk cost of polishing makes authors reluctant to change targets. The memo is shared with co-authors and, where possible, with one senior colleague who has published in the target journal. It is not sent to the editor.

Further reading on DrDPKlass: the Research Series notes on writing a contribution paragraph and on writing an identification section supply the sentences that the memo tests for fit. The memo does not replace those sections; it checks whether those sections will be legible to the specific editorial board.

How to Read a Target Journal’s Recent Contents for Fit

The practical way to answer the first question is to read the last twelve issues, or the last eighteen months of online-first papers, with a simple coding sheet. For each paper record the primary economic question, the main identification strategy, the country or countries of the sample, the data frequency, and the institutional feature that the paper treats as central. Do not read the abstracts only. The introduction and the first two pages of the methods tell you what the editor and reviewers actually accepted.

Three patterns usually appear. The first is a dominant method. A journal may publish many papers that use high-dimensional fixed effects and staggered difference-in-differences, or almost none. If your paper’s main claim rests on a regression discontinuity around a regulatory threshold, and the journal has published only two such designs in three years, the fit is thin unless the economic question is central. The second pattern is country or region concentration. Some journals that appear open to emerging markets in practice publish mostly Chinese or East-Asian samples, or multi-country panels that include India as one of thirty countries. A single-country Indian paper must then clear a higher bar on mechanism or identification. The third pattern is topical clustering. A journal that has published four papers on bank ownership and lending in the last two years is a different fit from a journal that has published none.

Indian scholars should also check the editorial board and recent handling editors. If the handling editors for empirical corporate finance have published extensively on U.S. governance and rarely on emerging-market regulation, the probability that an Indian regulatory-shock paper reaches a sympathetic desk is lower. This is expertise, not bias. The memo records the observation so the team can decide whether to target a journal whose handling editors have closer exposure to the institutional setting.

Callout. Aims-and-scope statements are marketing. The last eighteen months of published papers are the actual product. Code the product.

Mapping Your Contribution Against the Journal’s Stated and Unstated Preferences

Once the coding sheet exists, map the paper’s contribution onto it. The map has three columns: what the journal has recently rewarded, what our paper supplies, and the residual risk. The residual risk is the most likely fit-based desk-reject reason. For a paper that uses the staggered rollout of the 2015 SEBI LODR related-party amendment, the map for Emerging Markets Review might look as follows:

  • Rewarded: single-country emerging-market regulatory shocks that speak to a governance mechanism; difference-in-differences with firm and year fixed effects; discussion of how the institutional rule differs from U.S. or European analogues.
  • Supplied: staggered implementation, firm-level related-party transaction data from annual reports, and a mechanism test that separates tunnelling from legitimate related-party financing.
  • Residual risk: the journal has published several similar staggered-shock papers; the incremental contribution must be visible in the first page or the editor will treat the paper as another LODR study.

The same paper mapped to the Journal of Corporate Finance might show a different residual risk: the journal publishes fewer single-country papers and expects the mechanism to be portable. The memo then asks whether the introduction already contains a sentence that states the portable implication, or whether that sentence still needs to be written. If the sentence cannot be written without stretching the evidence, the residual risk is high and the memo recommends the better-matched outlet.

The map also reveals when the paper is stronger than the first-choice journal. A design that isolates a clean channel may be a better fit for a specialist journal than for a generalist outlet that will ask for additional robustness that the data cannot support. Early-career authors often reverse this logic and aim high first. The memo makes the trade-off explicit so that the team can choose deliberately rather than by default.

Handling the India-Specific Positioning Without Looking Parochial

Single-country Indian papers face a recurring fit objection: the institutional detail is interesting but the findings may not generalise. The memo must decide how the paper will answer that objection before the editor raises it. Three answers are credible. The first is that the Indian rule creates a measurement or identification opportunity that is rare elsewhere, and that the mechanism the paper isolates is theoretically portable. The second is that the paper speaks to a literature that already contains several single-country studies from other emerging markets, so the incremental India evidence is expected rather than exceptional. The third is that the paper’s contribution is methodological—an improved way to measure related-party transactions or to code a regulatory shock—and that the method can be applied in other settings.

The memo records which of these answers the paper can actually defend. If the paper can defend none of them cleanly, the fit for a generalist international journal is weak and the memo should recommend an emerging-markets or regional outlet where the India setting is a feature. This is not a downgrade. It is a match. A paper that is accepted and cited in Emerging Markets Review or the Pacific-Basin Finance Journal is more useful for an early-career Indian scholar than a paper that sits for eight months at a generalist journal and is then desk-rejected for fit.

Citation practice inside the paper must match the decision. If the memo chooses an emerging-markets journal, the literature review should engage the recent papers in that journal on related topics. If the memo chooses a generalist journal, the literature review should show the portable mechanism first and the Indian institutional detail second. Writing the review before the memo produces a review that fits neither audience cleanly.

Callout. “India is under-studied” is not a fit argument. The memo must state what the Indian setting allows the paper to observe that other settings do not.

The One-Page Memo Template and How to Use It with Co-Authors

The template is deliberately short so that it is actually written. Use the following headings and keep the total length to one page:

  1. Target journal and date of review of contents. Name the journal and the window of issues or online-first papers that were coded.
  2. Conversation currently running. Three to five bullet points drawn from the coding sheet: dominant methods, country concentration, topical clusters.
  3. Where our paper enters. One short paragraph that names the two or three recent papers a reviewer would be expected to cite and states the precise increment.
  4. Most likely fit-based desk-reject reason. One sentence. If you cannot write it, you have not yet understood the journal.
  5. Sentence that must be true. Quote or draft the single sentence in the introduction that carries the fit claim, and note the table or figure that defends it.
  6. Alternative if residual risk is high. Name the next-best journal and the one change in positioning that would be required.

Circulate the memo to co-authors with a deadline. Ask each co-author to mark the residual-risk sentence as “agree,” “disagree—evidence stronger,” or “disagree—evidence weaker.” Disagreement is useful. It surfaces whether the team has a shared view of the contribution before the cover letter is written. If the senior colleague who has published in the target journal marks the residual risk as high, treat that mark as information rather than as an obstacle. Adjust the target or adjust the introduction; do not ignore the mark.

Once the memo is agreed, the cover letter becomes easier to write. The cover letter can open with the conversation the journal is running and the precise increment, because those sentences have already been tested. The introduction can be checked against the “sentence that must be true.” The literature review can be trimmed to the papers that the memo identified as central. The memo therefore reduces work rather than adding it.

Common Failure Modes When the Memo Is Skipped

Papers that skip the memo tend to fail in predictable ways. The first failure is the prestige-first sequence: the paper is sent to the highest-ranked journal on the list regardless of recent contents, receives a desk reject that cites lack of general interest, and is then sent, with only cosmetic changes, to the next journal. The second failure is the citation mismatch: the literature review is dense with papers from Journal A while the submission goes to Journal B, so the editor cannot see the conversation. The third failure is the unstated residual risk: the authors know that the identification is thinner than the journal usually accepts, but they hope the institutional novelty will compensate. The editor does not share the hope.

A fourth failure is common among multi-author teams that include both early-career and senior scholars. The early-career author writes the memo and recommends a well-matched specialist journal. The senior author overrides the recommendation because a higher-ranked journal would look better on the early-career author’s CV. The paper is desk-rejected, the early-career author loses six months, and the senior author is only mildly inconvenienced. The memo makes the override visible and therefore discussable. It does not prevent the override, but it records the reasoning so that the team can learn from the outcome.

The same discipline applies when a paper is rejected and must be repositioned. Re-run the coding sheet for the next target before rewriting the introduction. The residual-risk sentence will usually change. If it does not, the second submission is likely to meet the same fate for the same reason.

Closing Field Checklist

Before the cover letter is drafted, confirm the following:

  • The coding sheet for the target journal covers at least the last twelve to eighteen months of published and online-first papers.
  • The memo names the two or three recent papers a reviewer would be expected to cite.
  • The residual-risk sentence has been written and has been marked by every co-author.
  • The “sentence that must be true” appears, in substance, in the current introduction and is defended by a table or figure already in the paper.
  • If the residual risk is high, an alternative journal has been named and the one positioning change required has been identified.
  • The literature-review citations have been checked against the papers the memo identified as central to the target journal’s conversation.
  • A senior colleague who has published in the target journal, or in a close analogue, has seen the memo.

The memo will not guarantee acceptance. It will remove the subset of desk rejects that occur because the paper was sent to an audience that was never going to recognise the contribution. For early-career Indian finance scholars under teaching loads and promotion clocks, that subset is large enough to justify the one page.
