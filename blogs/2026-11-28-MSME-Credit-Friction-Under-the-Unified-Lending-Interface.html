MSME Credit Friction under the Unified Lending Interface: Evidence Bankers and Scholars Need Before the Next Cycle

On 27 November 2026 RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra told the SME Finance & Investment Summit that bank credit to MSMEs had grown 27 per cent year-on-year in September 2026, far ahead of overall bank credit growth of 19.4 per cent, and that outstanding MSME credit had risen from ₹17.97 lakh crore in September 2021 to ₹42.89 lakh crore five years later. He was clear that the credit gap has narrowed but has not disappeared, and that attention must now shift to first-time borrowers, micro enterprises, women-led businesses and units in smaller towns and rural areas. The instrument he singled out for the next phase of friction reduction is the Unified Lending Interface (ULI), built by the Reserve Bank Innovation Hub, working with the Account Aggregator (AA) framework that already facilitated roughly ₹3.5 lakh crore of loans in FY 2025-26.

The Governor’s comparison was explicit: UPI changed how Indians make payments; ULI is expected to change how Indians obtain credit by pooling GST returns, bank statements, land and utility records so that documentation falls and assessment becomes faster and more data-based. For scholars who estimate MSME credit constraints and for bankers who price or prioritise first-time and micro borrowers, this is not background colour. It is a new infrastructure layer whose effect on application drop-off, time-to-decision and residual gaps must be measured before the next credit cycle hardens the new status quo. This paper supplies a short, practical protocol for that measurement.

Further reading on DrDPKlass: earlier notes on reading the RBI Financial Inclusion Index before estimating regional credit-access gaps and on reading an ECLGS notification before estimating MSME credit constraints set the broader data and identification context; this paper focuses on the specific friction points that ULI and the AA network are intended to remove.

Why the Unified Lending Interface is the next measurable friction point

Two facts from the Governor’s speech frame the measurement task. First, the volume of MSME credit has expanded rapidly, so aggregate growth rates alone no longer tell us where residual constraints bind. Second, the remaining constraints are concentrated among borrowers whose formal credit histories are short or non-existent. Traditional bureau scores and collateral requirements have always been least informative precisely for this group. The doubling of the collateral-free limit for micro and small enterprises from ₹10 lakh to ₹20 lakh (effective 1 April 2026) removes one barrier; it does not automatically create the information that lets a lender assess the enterprise’s present cash flows and growth potential.

ULI is designed to supply that information by bringing together data that already exist but are scattered—GST returns, bank-account statements via Account Aggregators, land records, utility payments—under a consent architecture. The practical claim is that an enterprise with a strong but short operating history can now be assessed without repeated branch visits and without inventing a credit history it does not yet have. Whether that claim is realised depends on three measurable frictions: (1) the share of first-time and micro applicants who complete a ULI-enabled application rather than dropping off at the documentation stage, (2) the reduction in calendar days from first consent to sanctioned or declined decision, and (3) the change in approval rates and subsequent delinquency for borrowers who would previously have been rejected for lack of traditional collateral or bureau history.

Measurement test: If a credit team or a researcher cannot, for a defined cohort of first-time or micro applicants, report the share that used an AA/ULI data pull, the median time from consent to decision, and the approval rate relative to a pre-ULI or non-ULI control, the infrastructure’s effect on friction remains an untested assertion.

The same logic applies to regional and gender dimensions. The Financial Inclusion Index already shows large gaps across states and between urban and rural districts. ULI reduces only the information component of those gaps. Scholars who treat “no formal credit history” as a binary constraint without observing whether an AA/ULI path was available and used will mis-attribute residual exclusion to borrower characteristics rather than to incomplete infrastructure rollout.

What ULI and Account Aggregator actually change for first-time borrowers

The Account Aggregator framework already allows a borrower to share financial information with a lender under explicit consent. The recent decision to bring forward the interoperability deadline to 1 December 2026 means a customer who onboards with one AA can have data pulled through the network rather than being limited to the bilateral ties of a single aggregator. That removes one practical drop-off point: the lender and the borrower’s bank no longer need a direct technical partnership for the pull to succeed.

ULI sits on top of this and other public and private data sources. According to the Governor, it pools GST returns, bank statements, land and utility records so that the lender can form a view of current activity and repayment capacity without demanding a thick physical file. For a first-time borrower the change is qualitative. Previously the application stalled when the branch asked for three years of audited accounts or a property title that the micro enterprise did not possess. Under a functioning ULI path the same enterprise can authorise a pull of recent GST filings and bank-statement cash-flow patterns; the lender’s model or credit officer then sees operating reality rather than the absence of a traditional file.

Three concrete changes follow. Documentation volume falls for the subset of applicants who have GST or bank-account activity that can be pulled. Time-to-decision compresses because the data arrive digitally rather than by courier or branch visit. And the assessment shifts, at the margin, from “does this borrower have collateral or a bureau score?” to “does the recent operating data support the requested limit?” None of these changes is automatic. They occur only for applicants who (a) have the underlying data, (b) successfully complete the consent journey, and (c) encounter a lender whose systems and credit policy actually consume the ULI/AA output.

A practical friction-measurement protocol for banks and researchers

A usable protocol fits on one page and can be run monthly by a credit-operations team or quarterly by a researcher with access to anonymised application logs. It has four steps.

  1. Define the cohort. First-time borrowers (no prior formal credit with the institution or, where data allow, no bureau history) and micro enterprises (turnover or investment below the current MSME threshold). Stratify by gender of the primary applicant, by district type (metro / other urban / rural), and by whether the applicant has an active GSTIN.
  2. Tag the data path. For every application record whether an AA pull was attempted, whether it succeeded, and whether ULI-sourced fields (GST, land, utility) entered the decision file. Keep a simple three-way flag: traditional only, AA only, AA+ULI.
  3. Capture three friction metrics. (i) Completion rate: share of started applications that reach a decision. (ii) Median calendar days from first consent (or first document upload) to decision. (iii) Approval rate and, for approved cases, the share that later become 30+ days past due within six months.
  4. Compare within the same period. Contrast the three metrics across the traditional, AA-only and AA+ULI paths inside the same cohort and month. A pre-period comparison is useful but not required; the contemporaneous path comparison already isolates the infrastructure effect from macro credit-cycle effects.

Indian credit teams that have piloted similar tags find that the largest drop-off still occurs before the AA consent screen is even shown—usually because the relationship manager does not offer the digital path or the applicant’s phone number is not linked to the bank account. Recording that pre-consent failure separately prevents the infrastructure from being blamed for a process failure that sits upstream.

Practical tip: Store the monthly friction dashboard in the same controlled folder as the priority-sector and MSME reporting pack. A one-line note on the cover page (“ULI/AA path used in X per cent of first-time micro applications this month; median decision time Y days”) turns the measurement into a standing management item rather than a one-off study.

Consent, data and identification boundaries that must be respected

Any measurement that uses AA or ULI data must respect the consent architecture that makes the infrastructure legal. Researchers and internal analytics teams cannot treat a successful data pull as a free research dataset. The practical boundaries are:

  • Consent is purpose-specific and time-bound. An application-level pull does not authorise later research use of the same raw statements or GST returns. Aggregated, anonymised friction metrics are the safe output; raw files are not.
  • Identification remains at the application or borrower-token level. Linking ULI outcomes to external administrative data (for example, GSTIN-level turnover) requires a separate, documented legal basis; it cannot be assumed.
  • Model or credit-policy changes that consume ULI fields must be versioned. A researcher who estimates “the effect of ULI” without knowing whether the lender’s scorecard was updated in the same month will confound infrastructure with underwriting-rule changes.
  • Women-led and rural cohorts are small. Report confidence intervals or suppress cells below a minimum count so that the measurement itself does not create privacy risk.

These boundaries are not obstacles to useful evidence. They simply force the output to be the friction metrics listed above rather than a dump of individual cash-flow series. Controllers and compliance teams already know how to enforce them; the measurement protocol should be reviewed by them before the first monthly run.

Implementation checklist for credit teams and NBFC partners

Banks and NBFCs that wish to move from aspiration to evidence can complete the following in one quarter:

  • Map every current first-time and micro product to the three data-path flags (traditional / AA / AA+ULI). Confirm with the technology team that the flags are written to the application log at the moment of decision, not reconstructed later.
  • Train relationship managers and digital-channel prompts to offer the AA/ULI path as the default for any applicant who has a linked bank account or GSTIN. Record the reason when the path is not offered.
  • Set a monthly dashboard owner (usually the MSME credit head or the priority-sector cell) who publishes the three friction metrics stratified by the cohort definitions above.
  • Agree with internal audit a simple re-performance test: sample twenty first-time applications, confirm the data-path flag matches the actual consent record, and confirm the decision-time stamp is accurate.
  • For NBFC partners that originate on the bank’s behalf, require the same three flags in the monthly data feed. Without them the bank cannot see whether its own ULI investment is reaching the borrowers the Governor highlighted.

Teams that treat the checklist as a compliance exercise rather than a management tool usually discover, within two months, that the binding constraint is not the technology but the share of applications on which the digital path is never offered. That discovery is itself valuable; it tells the credit committee where process redesign will move the friction numbers faster than further infrastructure spend.

Implications for residual credit-gap and regional-access estimates

Scholars who estimate MSME credit gaps or regional access differentials now have an additional observable. The residual gap after controlling for firm size, sector and location can be further decomposed into the share of applicants for whom an AA/ULI path was available and used versus the share for whom it was not. If the approval-rate differential between AA+ULI and traditional paths is large and stable, part of what earlier papers attributed to “information asymmetry” or “collateral scarcity” is in fact incomplete rollout of the new infrastructure.

The same decomposition matters for the Financial Inclusion Index sub-components that track credit access. An improvement in the index that coincides with rising ULI usage in previously low-access districts is more informative than an improvement that occurs only in districts where traditional branch density was already high. Researchers who continue to use only district-level aggregate credit outstanding will miss this margin. The practical recommendation is to request, or to construct from application logs where available, the three friction metrics at district or state level for first-time and micro cohorts, then to interact those metrics with the usual supply and demand controls.

For women-led enterprises the measurement is especially sharp. The Governor explicitly named them as a priority. If the AA/ULI completion rate or approval rate for women-led first-time applicants remains materially lower than for male-led applicants inside the same district and product, the infrastructure has not yet equalised the information playing field; further product or process design is required. Publishing that differential, even if small samples force wide confidence intervals, prevents the aggregate MSME growth numbers from masking a persistent gender gap.

Risks, open questions and what remains unmeasured

Several risks are already visible. Interoperability of the AA network, while brought forward to 1 December 2026, still depends on every FIP and FIU actually completing the technical work; partial compliance will recreate the bilateral drop-off the change was meant to eliminate. ULI’s value is highest for enterprises that already generate digital trails (GST, bank statements). Purely cash-based micro units remain outside its reach; measuring only the digital subset will overstate the infrastructure’s coverage of the Governor’s target group. Credit-policy inertia is another risk: a lender can receive rich ULI data and still apply the old collateral rule, so the data path flag must be read together with the actual decision rule version.

Open questions that the monthly protocol will not answer by itself include the long-run delinquency of ULI-approved first-time borrowers relative to traditional approvals and the extent to which faster decisions simply accelerate adverse selection. Those questions require longer panels; they are the natural next papers once the basic friction metrics exist.

What remains unmeasured today, in most institutions, is precisely the set of three metrics listed in the protocol. Until those numbers are produced routinely, claims that ULI has reduced MSME credit friction stay at the level of infrastructure announcement rather than demonstrated operating improvement. The Governor’s 27 November speech sets the policy intent. The measurement protocol in this paper is the minimum evidence standard that lets bankers manage to that intent and lets scholars test it.

Policy Analysis Series checklist

  • Cohort of first-time and micro applicants defined and stratified by gender, district type and GSTIN status.
  • Three data-path flags (traditional / AA / AA+ULI) written to the application log at decision time.
  • Monthly dashboard of completion rate, median decision days and approval rate by path and cohort.
  • Consent and privacy review completed before any analytics run; only aggregated metrics retained.
  • Internal-audit re-performance test agreed and scheduled.
  • NBFC partner feeds required to carry the same flags.
  • Residual credit-gap or FI-index work updated to interact with observed ULI/AA usage.
  • Pre-consent drop-off (path never offered) recorded separately from infrastructure failure.
