How Indian Finance Scholars Should Tell a Tough Journal from a Predatory Outlet Before They Upload

A desk rejection from a serious finance journal is expensive in time and useful in information. An acceptance from a predatory outlet is cheap in time and expensive in reputation. Indian early-career scholars, doctoral candidates facing a submission clause, and faculty chasing an API or CAS point often confuse the two, because both experiences arrive as emails and both websites now look respectable.

This Research Series briefing is a pre-upload protocol. It is not a blacklist and it is not a ranking of Indian or international titles. Lists go stale, publishers rebrand, and a journal that is merely selective can look hostile if you have only read its aims-and-scope paragraph. The test below asks whether an outlet has a living conversation, a named editorial process, and a fee logic you can explain to a doctoral committee. If you cannot pass that test in ninety minutes, do not upload.

1. Why the upload is a career decision, not a form

Most submission systems ask for a PDF, a cover letter, and a declaration that the paper is not under review elsewhere. They do not ask the question that matters: will this outlet’s name still be defensible when a selection committee, a coauthor’s university, or a later editor reads the CV line? The pressure on Indian campuses is real. A supervisor wants an indexed paper before the pre-submission seminar. A college counts any ISSN toward appraisal. A group chat forwards an invitation with a discount if you pay this week.

Toughness and predation are not opposites on a single prestige scale. A tough journal can reject a competent paper because the conversation is wrong, the identification is thin, or the contribution paragraph restates the setting as the finding. A predatory outlet can accept a competent paper because the business model is volume, not selection. Treating every rejection as proof of quality, and every acceptance as proof of merit, is how CVs fill with titles that later have to be explained away.

Working distinction. A tough journal spends editorial time to refuse most manuscripts and still publishes a traceable conversation. A predatory outlet spends marketing time to accept most manuscripts and publishes a pile that does not talk to itself.

The cost of a wrong upload is not only the article-processing charge. It is the months the file cannot be sent elsewhere if the outlet claims copyright, the awkward line in a faculty application, and the signal you send coauthors about your standards. Withdrawal is possible. It is slower than never uploading.

2. What a tough journal actually looks like

Scholars often describe toughness as a long wait or a rude reviewer. Those are symptoms, not the institution. A tough finance or accounting outlet, whether based in India or abroad, has four observable properties you can check without an insider.

2.1 A conversation, not a topic cloud

Open the last eight issues, not the homepage banner. Can you name the debates the journal actually hosts—payout policy under a governance reform, bank risk after a regulatory change, disclosure quality and cost of capital, audit effort and reporting lags? A tough journal’s recent papers cite one another and cite a shared set of methods papers. A topic cloud accepts “finance,” “management,” and “multidisciplinary” in the same issue with no shared references. India as a sample is not a conversation. A claim about how an Indian institutional feature changes a known relation can be.

2.2 Named people who can refuse you

Editors and associate editors should be identifiable scholars with verifiable affiliations and a publication record in the field the journal claims. Cross-check two or three names on university pages, not only on the journal site. A masthead of fifty “editorial board members” from unrelated disciplines, with no handling editor named in decision letters, is a brochure. Tough journals also show desk-reject behaviour: many papers never reach reviewers. That is selection, not hostility.

2.3 A process you can reconstruct

Author guidelines should state what is reviewed, by whom in role if not by name, how revisions are handled, and what the data or ethics statement must contain. Decision letters from a tough journal refer to the paper’s claim, tables, and threats. They do not arrive in forty-eight hours with praise, a formatted PDF, and an invoice. Slow is not automatically serious—some serious journals are slow because of referee load—but instant acceptance after a generic review is not seriousness.

2.4 A fee logic that matches the product

Subscription journals may charge nothing to authors. Open-access journals may charge after acceptance, with a waiver policy a public-university scholar in India can actually use. The fee, if any, should be stated before submission, tied to publication rather than to “review,” and issued by a publisher you can invoice through an institution. A demand for payment to start review, a personal UPI handle, or a discount that expires on Friday is not an article-processing charge. It is a sales close.

Do. Read three accepted papers from the last two years and write one sentence on why your manuscript belongs in that conversation. If the sentence is only “it is Scopus” or “the impact factor is acceptable for API,” you have not done a fit test.

3. Predatory signals that survive a polished site

The old tells—broken English, a stock photo of a campus that is not theirs, an ISSN that does not resolve—still appear. They are no longer sufficient. Many questionable outlets now have SSL certificates, DOI prefixes, and a page that name-drops indexing. Use a cluster of signals. One oddity can be a small society journal with a volunteer webmaster. Four oddities are a business.

SignalWhat you should see if it is seriousWhat should stop the upload
InvitationA call that names your paper’s claim, or a standing special issue with a guest editor you can verifyA blast email praising your “eminent profile,” promising publication in days, or offering a waiver code
ScopeA field narrow enough that an associate editor could referee the method“All areas of business, engineering, humanities and medical science” on one title
Index claimsA database record you open yourself and match to the ISSN and publisherA badge for Scopus, Web of Science, or UGC-CARE that you cannot find on the database’s own site
BoardScholars who list the role on their own university pageNames that do not resolve, or scholars who say they never consented
ArchiveStable volumes, retractions when needed, and papers that cite prior issuesHundreds of papers a month, dead links, or an archive that resets after a rebrand
MoneyA published fee schedule, institutional invoice, and waiver rulePayment to review, a personal account, or a fee revealed only after acceptance

Indexing is the trap that catches careful people. UGC-CARE, Scopus, and Web of Science are not interchangeable, and presence on a list is not a substitute for reading the journal. Lists are revised. A title can be indexed and still be a poor fit. A title can display a logo and not be indexed at all. Open the database, search the ISSN, and note the coverage year. If the journal’s website claims a quartile the database does not show, treat the claim as a finding about the outlet, not as a clerical error you should forgive.

Do not. Use a random “predatory list” screenshot from a coaching channel as your only evidence. Some lists are outdated, some conflate open access with predation, and some are themselves commercial. Your file note should cite what you checked: archive, board, fee page, and the indexer’s own record.

Language quality is a weak signal in both directions. A tough journal publishing emerging-economy work may still have uneven copy-editing. A predatory outlet may buy clean templates. If consecutive papers reuse the same generic regression paragraph with swapped variable names, you are looking at a mill.

4. The grey zone: weak legitimate, hijacked, and special-issue mills

Binary labels fail in the middle, which is where most early-career mistakes happen. Separate three neighbours of predation.

4.1 Weak but legitimate

A society journal, a new university title, or a regional accounting review can be real: named editors, slow issues, no surprise fee, and a small conversation. It may not help a shortlist at a research-intensive department. It may still be an honest home for a teaching note or a descriptive Indian institutional paper that a field journal will not take. The error is not publishing there. The error is describing it on a CV as equivalent to a selective field journal, or paying a broker to “get it in.” If you choose it, say why in your own notes: audience, turnaround you can live with, and no false index claim.

4.2 Hijacked titles

Hijacking copies the name, ISSN, or look of a real journal on a different domain. The tell is duplication. Search the journal title in quotes. If two sites claim the same volume and one asks for payment to a new “online system,” stop. Email the editor listed on the society or university page you already trust, not the address in the solicitation. Indian scholars have lost files this way because the real journal’s name was familiar from a supervisor’s CV.

4.3 Special-issue mills

Guest-edited issues are a normal part of serious publishing when the guest editor is a scholar, the call states a question, and the parent journal’s process still applies. They become mills when the parent title is broad, the guest editor is a conference organiser you cannot place in the literature, and acceptance is bundled with a conference fee or a “publication package.” A special issue on “digital transformation and sustainability in emerging markets” that will take forty papers is not a conversation. It is inventory.

Keep considering

Named handling editor, two years of coherent issues, fee published or absent, index claim you verified, papers you would cite.

Stop

Unverified index badge, pay-to-review, hijacked domain, board that does not list the role, archive that does not cite itself.

Conference proceedings sit in the same grey zone. A selective association proceeding with a discussant can be a pipeline step. A proceeding that promises a later journal slot if you pay a second invoice is a funnel. Treat the proceeding and the journal as separate decisions.

5. A ninety-minute fit test before you upload

Do this once per outlet, on a weekday when you are not answering the invitation’s deadline. Predatory timelines are designed to expire before the test.

  1. Name the conversation (15 minutes). From the last eight issues, list six papers you could cite in your related-work section. If you cannot, the outlet is not your conversation, however indexed it claims to be.
  2. Verify the people (15 minutes). Pick the editor-in-chief and one associate or guest editor. Find each on a university or society page that is not the journal’s site. Note mismatches.
  3. Verify the index claim (15 minutes). Open the database the journal cites. Match ISSN, publisher, and years. Screenshot or save the record in your project folder. A logo on the journal site is not a record.
  4. Read the money page (10 minutes). Who is paid, when, by what invoice, and what happens if you withdraw after review. If the page is missing and an email asks for a transfer, stop.
  5. Read one methods section and one decision path (20 minutes). Is the empirical work specific enough that a referee could challenge it? Does the guide describe revision, or only formatting? Search for retractions or expressions of concern linked to the title.
  6. Write the fit sentence (15 minutes). One sentence: what this journal has published that your paper extends, and what it will likely reject you for. If you cannot name a likely reject reason, you have not met a tough journal. Tough journals have reject reasons. Mills have templates.
A quartile screenshot is not a fit sentence. A fit sentence names a debate, a method the outlet already knows, and a reason they might still desk-reject you.

Keep the note with the manuscript. When a coauthor or a doctoral committee asks why this outlet, you should be able to forward six titles, the index record, and the fee rule. “Everyone in the department uses it” is not a note. Departments develop bad habits when the first person is not checked and the next ten copy the CV line.

For Indian public-university scholars, add one institutional check. Will the finance office pay an article charge only against a published schedule and a GST invoice? If the outlet cannot meet that bar, you will either pay personally or stall after acceptance. For scholars targeting a later international submission, check whether the copyright assignment will block a revised version elsewhere.

API and CAS caution. A point that depends on an index can disappear if the index drops the title. Build the CV line you can defend after a list revision: the claim of the paper, the outlet’s conversation, and the process you documented. Points are a consequence. They are a poor primary screen.

6. What to do if the file has already gone

If you uploaded before the test, do not hope the email was a misunderstanding. Reconstruct the six checks now. Three outcomes are common.

The outlet fails the cluster. Write to the editor and withdraw, stating that the manuscript is no longer submitted and must not be published. Keep the sent mail. If a fee was taken, ask the institution’s accounts office before you dispute a personal transfer; do not send a second payment to “unlock” withdrawal. Tell coauthors the same day. Do not cite the forthcoming version in a thesis synopsis.

The outlet is weak but real. Decide whether the conversation is still the right home. If you stay, revise as you would for a tougher desk: contribution paragraph, identification threats, data note. If you leave, withdraw before acceptance so the file is free. A published weak paper is not fatal. Pretending it was a field-journal acceptance is.

You are inside a hijacked site. Contact the real journal’s editorial office through the society or publisher domain, not the link in the spam mail. Assume the file may have been exposed. Do not reuse confidential firm data in the same form without checking your licence and consent. Then submit only after the ninety-minute test, to an outlet whose domain matches the publisher of record.

Supervisors should not outsource this test to the candidate alone. A clause that says “one indexed paper before the thesis” without a fit conversation pushes students toward the invitation in their inbox. Ask to see the six citeable papers and the index record before you sign a no-objection note.

Do not respond to a second outlet’s offer to “transfer” the manuscript inside a publisher family you have not checked title by title. Cascading transfer can be legitimate inside a real publisher. It is also how a rejected file is walked into a weaker title with a fee. Repeat the fit sentence for the destination title.

7. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this list on the day you are tempted to upload. If any “stop” item is true, the manuscript stays on your machine.

  • I can name six papers from the last two years that this outlet published in my conversation, and I would cite at least three.
  • The editor I would write to is a real scholar on a page that is not the journal’s own site.
  • Every index or UGC-CARE claim has been opened on the indexer’s site and matched to ISSN and publisher.
  • The fee, if any, is published, invoiced to an institution, and charged for publication rather than for review.
  • The archive cites itself. The scope can refuse a paper. The website is not a copy of a better-known title.
  • I have a fit sentence that includes a likely reason for desk rejection.
  • Coauthors have seen the note. A doctoral committee will not be surprised by the outlet’s name.
  • I am not uploading because a discount expires this week.

Tough journals will still reject you. That rejection is evidence about fit, identification, or contribution, and it belongs in a pipeline that already has a second and third outlet chosen by the same test. Predatory acceptance is evidence about the outlet. It does not become evidence about the paper. Indian finance scholarship does not need more PDFs. It needs manuscripts that enter conversations a later reader can find, challenge, and cite.

Before the next invitation, spend the ninety minutes. The upload button will still be there. The CV line will not be as easy to move.
