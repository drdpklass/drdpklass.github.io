How to Give Written Feedback First-Generation Commerce Students Will Actually Use

Most written comments on Indian commerce scripts never become a next draft. The teacher spends a Sunday circling “vague,” “explain,” and “poor English.” The student photographs the first page, files the PDF, and writes the next assignment in the same pattern. This briefing is a working method for giving fewer comments that first-generation B.Com., BBA and MBA students can actually use before the next class. It is written for faculty who mark 60 to 180 scripts a fortnight and still want writing to improve.

The setting is the large Indian classroom after NEP’s talk of continuous assessment, after NAAC’s interest in outcome-based education, and after the quiet arrival of generative tools that can produce a fluent paragraph that does not understand working capital. The same method travels to other emerging-economy campuses where English is a medium of examination rather than a home language, and where the first person in the family to sit a university exam has no editor at the dining table.

1. Why the red-pen ritual fails in a 60-student section

The ritual looks conscientious. Every margin has ink. Every grammatical slip is marked. The last line says “Good attempt. Improve presentation.” The student cannot reconstruct, from that page, which move would raise the mark by one band. Presentation is not a move. “Improve English” is not a move. A circle around a sentence is a diagnosis without a prescription.

Three structural facts make the ritual worse in Indian commerce programmes. First, class size. A teacher who writes eight comments on 80 scripts has produced 640 remarks. Almost none will be reread with a pen in hand. Second, language distance. Many students think in Odia, Hindi, Telugu or Bengali and write examination English under time pressure. Surface error is real, but it is rarely the binding constraint on a weak answer; the binding constraint is that the claim has no object, no comparison and no number. Third, the assessment calendar. Internal tests stack. There is no week between scripts for a conference. Feedback that assumes a tutorial culture imported from a 15-student seminar will die on the notice board.

Working rule. Feedback is a sentence a student can act on in the next sitting, tied to one criterion that already appears on the rubric. Everything else is annotation for the teacher’s conscience.

The failure is not kindness. Many Indian teachers mark late at night because they care. The failure is design. Comments are treated as proof of labour rather than as an instructional technology with a capacity limit.

2. What first-generation commerce writers are actually doing

First-generation does not mean weak. It means the student has no inherited template for what a “discussion” looks like in a university answer. In school they were rewarded for completeness: list four points, underline headings, fill the page. In a B.Com. paper on financial management or a first-year MBA case, completeness without a decision looks empty. The student does not know this until someone names the missing move.

Watch the typical script. The opening restates the question. The middle offers definitions copied from the textbook or from a coaching note. The close says “hence it is important for companies in the present scenario.” There is no firm, no year, no amount, no trade-off. When the teacher writes “give examples,” the next script adds Tata and Reliance with no figure attached. The student heard “brand names,” not “a comparison that can be false.”

Language anxiety compounds the problem. Students who fear grammar hide in safe nouns. They avoid verbs that commit: rises, lags, fails, concentrates, leaks. They write “there is an impact on profitability” because impact cannot be marked wrong. Feedback that attacks articles and prepositions first confirms their fear that English, not thinking, is the subject. The first-generation student then outsources fluency to a chatbot and submits a paragraph that is clean and empty. The teacher who only polices grammar has trained that outcome.

Do not. Open the comment bank with “Your English needs improvement.” If the claim is empty, say the claim is empty. If a tense error blocks meaning, mark that one sentence. A general language verdict teaches shame, not revision.

Also name the resource gap without pity. Many of these students work evenings, share a phone, and write on a bus. Feedback that requires a 40-minute office hour they cannot attend is not equitable. Feedback that fits a 20-minute rewrite in the library after class is.

3. Budget comments like a scarce resource

Decide the comment budget before you open the first script. For a 400-word internal answer, three comments is a generous ceiling: one strength that is specific, one priority change, one forward task. For a 2,000-word term paper, five is enough if they map to rubric rows. More ink does not raise learning. It raises the chance that the student reads none of it.

Script typeComment budgetWhat each comment must do
10-mark internal2–3 sentences totalName the missing move; point to the sentence where it should have appeared.
Case memo (MBA)4 comments maxDecision quality, evidence, alternative rejected, next number to compute.
Term paper5 comments + rubric ticksOne per criterion; no extra grammar tour unless meaning collapses.
Project reportA one-page cover noteWhat to keep, what to cut, what table is missing, by when.

Batch the work. Mark ten scripts for the priority change only. Then mark the next ten. Mixing “I will just fix this comma” with “I will rethink this argument” is how Sundays disappear. If a pattern repeats in twelve scripts—definition dump, no number, no alternative—write one class comment and stop repeating it in margins. A five-minute opening in the next lecture is cheaper and more public than twelve identical paragraphs.

Spend ink here

The sentence that should have contained a comparison, a number, or a rejected alternative. The paragraph that restates the question. The table with no unit.

Do not spend ink here

Every article. Decorative headings. A misspelling that does not change the claim. Praise that could fit any script in the pile.

4. Write verbs a student can execute before the next class

Comments fail when they describe the teacher’s feeling rather than the student’s next action. “Unclear,” “weak analysis,” “could be better structured” are moods. Replace them with verbs that survive a bus ride: name, compare, cut, compute, locate, reject, sequence.

4.1 The three-line comment

Use one stable shape so students learn to look for it.

  1. Locate. “In paragraph 2, after the definition of current ratio…”
  2. Name the missing move. “…you never say whether the firm is tighter or looser than last year or than a peer.”
  3. Task. “Rewrite that paragraph with two numbers and one sentence that starts ‘Compared with…’.”

That block is longer than “explain more,” and shorter than a lecture. It can be reused with different locations. Students start hunting for the third line. That is the point.

4.2 Forbidden and allowed stems

Retire these stemsUse these stems
Needs more depthAdd the 2024 figure from the annual report and say what moved.
Be more criticalReject one textbook reason that does not fit this firm.

Language issueThis sentence has two subjects. Keep the firm as the subject.
If the student cannot do the task in twenty minutes without you in the room, the comment is still a lecture. Shorten the task until it fits the calendar they actually have.

5. Attach feedback to one visible criterion, not to taste

First-generation students treat marks as weather. A 6/10 after a 8/10 feels like luck. The only antidote is a rubric they saw before they wrote, with the comment pointing at one row of that rubric. If the criterion was “uses a comparison that could be false,” the comment should quote that row. Taste comments—“I expected a more mature tone”—train students to guess the teacher rather than to practise a move.

Keep the rubric short enough to print on the question paper. Four rows beat nine. For a commerce short answer, a usable set is: (1) names an object and a period, (2) uses at least one number with a unit, (3) makes a comparison or states a trade-off, (4) writes in complete claims rather than heading-and-list. Language can sit inside row 4 as “the claim is readable,” not as a separate personality test.

Show one annotated script on the projector. Not the best script—the mid script. Walk the three-line comment live. First-generation students need to see that a 6 becomes a 7 by adding a comparison, not by buying a new pen. If you only display toppers, you teach that writing quality is a trait.

Do. Return the rubric with ticks and one written task. A grid without a sentence still feels like a verdict. A sentence without a grid still feels like taste. Use both.

6. Close the loop with a 20-minute revision window

Feedback that cannot be used before the next grade is archive material. Build a loop that fits Indian timetables.

Same-week rewrite (preferred). In the next class, give twelve minutes. Students rewrite only the paragraph you pointed at. Collect that paragraph, not the whole answer. Mark it with a plus, a check or a minus against the one criterion. This is two hours of work for a 60-student section if you refuse to re-mark the original.

Peer swap with a constraint. Pairs exchange only the task line. The partner checks whether the new paragraph contains the required number or comparison. Peers are not editors of English here. They are checkers of a binary feature. That keeps status anxiety down and keeps the exercise from becoming a grammar court.

Portfolio of three moves. Across the semester, each student keeps one page: three before-and-after paragraphs. That page is the writing evidence for NAAC, for a parent, and for the student who thinks they “have poor English.” It shows a skill, not a trait.

Do not demand a full resubmission of every internal. Full resubmission is how plagiarism and chatbot reuse explode, and how your Sunday vanishes. Demand the one paragraph that was named. That is enough to teach the habit of revision.

Integrity note. If a rewrite arrives with sudden fluency and no new number, ask the student to explain the comparison aloud for sixty seconds. You are not running a tribunal. You are checking that the move lives in their head, not only in a paste buffer.

7. Protect the teaching load without going silent

Early-career faculty in Indian colleges already carry labs, invigilation, NAAC folders and a research file that is supposed to grow at night. A feedback system that requires a personal letter on every script will be abandoned by October. Design for the load you have in February, not the load you wish you had in the orientation week.

  • Comment only on the criterion that is the week’s teaching target.
  • Use a class-level comment when more than a third of scripts share the same hole.
  • Audio-note a sample of five scripts if handwriting time is the bottleneck; keep the three-line shape.
  • Refuse to mark late work with the same depth as on-time work. Depth is a reward for the calendar, not a punishment for the student who is struggling.
  • Tell the department, in writing, the comment budget. Otherwise a well-meaning HoD will treat sparse margins as neglect.

Silence is the other failure. Some teachers, exhausted, return only a number. First-generation students then invent a story: the mark was personal, the subject is not for them, English is the wall. A number plus one executable sentence is already a different institution.

Heads of department can help without new money. Standardise one short rubric for all first-year commerce papers. Reserve fifteen minutes of one staff meeting to compare three anonymised scripts. Stop asking faculty to write “detailed qualitative remarks” for every CIA if nobody has defined what detailed means. Detail without a budget is how good teachers burn out and how average comments multiply.

8. Field checklist and closing brief

Use this list before you return the next set of internals.

  • Did students see the rubric before they wrote.
  • Is the comment budget written down (two or three sentences, not “as needed”).
  • Does every comment locate a sentence, name a missing move, and assign a task that fits twenty minutes.
  • Have you retired “improve English,” “be more critical,” and “good attempt” as standalone remarks.
  • Is there a same-week window to use the task, even if it is twelve minutes in class.
  • Will you collect a paragraph, not a second full script.
  • Have you prepared one class-level comment for the pattern that appeared a dozen times.
  • Can a first-generation student explain, from your note alone, what would raise the mark by one band.
  • Have you protected Sunday by batching and by refusing a grammar tour.
  • Will one mid script be annotated live so the move is public.

Written feedback is not a personality. It is a small technology: a located task tied to a public criterion, with a slot on the timetable to use it. First-generation commerce students do not need more ink. They need one comparison they can write on a bus, one number with a unit, and a teacher who will look only at that sentence next week. That is a higher standard than a page of red circles. It is also the only standard a 60-student section can keep until March.

Teach the move. Budget the ink. Close the loop. The rest is decoration on a script that will never be opened again.
