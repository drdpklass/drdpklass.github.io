How Indian Banks Should Construct a Physical Climate Risk Heatmap for the Loan Book That Credit Committees Can Actually Use

Physical climate risk is no longer a sustainability-team exercise. For Indian banks it is already a credit problem: a monsoon failure that lifts NPA ratios in a district, a coastal inundation that impairs collateral values, or a heatwave that reduces industrial output and cash flow. Yet most loan-book heatmaps still live in Excel files that credit committees cannot interrogate, or in external consultant decks that arrive too late and at the wrong granularity. This briefing sets out a practical construction method that produces a heatmap a credit committee can actually use—one that sits inside the existing credit process rather than beside it. The method is deliberately low-technology. It uses data the bank already holds, public Indian hazard layers, and a scoring rule that can be explained in a single page. The goal is not a perfect climate model; it is a repeatable input that changes limits, pricing, or monitoring when the evidence warrants it.

Why physical climate risk belongs on the credit heat map, not in the sustainability report

Indian banks already run sector, geography, and rating heatmaps for concentration risk. Physical climate risk is simply another dimension of concentration: borrowers clustered in the same flood plain, the same drought-prone taluka, or the same heat-exposed industrial cluster. Treating it as a separate ESG topic creates two problems. First, the risk never enters the limit-setting or pricing conversation. Second, the sustainability team is asked to “assess climate risk” without access to the loan-level data that only the credit function holds.

The Reserve Bank of India has signalled, through its climate-risk discussion papers and subsequent supervisory expectations, that banks must identify and manage physical risks in their portfolios. The practical implication is straightforward: the heatmap must be owned by credit risk, fed by data the bank already possesses (branch, PIN code, sector, outstanding, collateral type), and refreshed at the same frequency as other concentration reports. A quarterly pack that arrives after the credit committee has already approved the largest exposures is decorative.

Callout. If the heatmap cannot change a limit, a pricing overlay, or a collateral haircut, it is not yet a credit tool.

Mapping Indian hazard data to bank geographies without inventing precision

India has usable public hazard layers. The India Meteorological Department publishes district-level rainfall anomalies and heatwave days. The National Disaster Management Authority and state disaster authorities maintain flood and cyclone vulnerability maps. The Central Water Commission tracks reservoir and river levels. These sources are imperfect—resolution is often district or block, not asset-level—but they are better than nothing and, crucially, they are free and updateable. For coastal states, state disaster management authorities often publish cyclone and storm-surge vulnerability at the block level. For the Indo-Gangetic plain, river-basin flood maps from the Central Water Commission and state irrigation departments are more relevant than national averages. Banks with significant agricultural exposure should also consult the Ministry of Agriculture’s drought manuals and the IMD’s standardised precipitation index series. The practical test is simple: can the source be cited, downloaded, and refreshed without a vendor contract? If the answer is yes, start there.

The construction sequence is:

  1. Geocode the loan book to PIN code or, where available, latitude-longitude of the primary operating location or collateral. For agricultural and MSME books this is usually the branch PIN; for larger corporates it is the plant or project location recorded in the credit appraisal memorandum.
  2. Overlay the hazard layer at the finest available administrative unit that covers at least 80 percent of the book. District is usually the practical ceiling; pretending to have village-level precision when the underlying map is district-level creates false confidence.
  3. Score the hazard on a simple ordinal scale (low / moderate / high / very high) using the source’s own categories or a transparent percentile of historical intensity (for example, rainfall deficit in the bottom quintile of the last 30 years). Document the exact rule so that next quarter’s refresh is reproducible.

Do not purchase a proprietary “climate score” and drop it into the system without understanding the underlying hazard model, the time horizon, and the scenario. Many commercial scores blend physical and transition risk, use global rather than Indian hazard data, and cannot be audited by the bank’s model-risk function. Start with public Indian sources; add commercial layers only after the internal methodology is stable.

Translating hazard intensity into sector and borrower vulnerability scores

A high flood hazard in a district does not automatically mean high credit risk. Vulnerability depends on the sector and the borrower’s adaptive capacity. Irrigated agriculture with diversified cropping is less exposed to a single monsoon failure than rain-fed single-crop farming. A steel plant on elevated land with captive power is less exposed to riverine flooding than a riverside textile unit. A commercial real-estate project with elevated plinth and insurance is less exposed than an informal industrial shed. In the Indian context, three additional factors matter. First, the presence of formal crop or asset insurance reduces effective vulnerability even when the physical hazard is high; the heatmap should therefore carry an insurance-coverage flag. Second, access to formal credit and irrigation infrastructure (for example, coverage under the Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana or state canal systems) improves adaptive capacity for agricultural borrowers. Third, for MSMEs, membership in a cluster with common effluent treatment or elevated infrastructure lowers the score relative to a stand-alone unit in the same hazard zone.

Build a simple vulnerability matrix:

  • Sector vulnerability (1–5). Agriculture (rain-fed) = 5; agriculture (irrigated/diversified) = 3; textiles and food processing in flood plains = 4; heavy industry with fixed plant = 3; services and IT = 1–2. Adjust for the specific Indian sub-sector. Cotton ginning in a drought district is not the same as cotton spinning in a port city.
  • Borrower adaptive capacity (1–5, inverted). Large corporates with insurance, geographic diversification, and liquid collateral score lower vulnerability. Small and marginal farmers, MSMEs without formal insurance, and borrowers whose entire cash flow depends on one location score higher.
  • Collateral sensitivity. Immovable collateral in a high-hazard zone receives a higher haircut suggestion; movable or insured collateral does not.

Multiply or add the scores according to a documented rule (for example, risk score = hazard × sector vulnerability × adaptive-capacity factor). Keep the formula transparent. Credit committees distrust black-box multiplications.

India-specific note. Many agricultural and priority-sector loans are already concentrated in a handful of districts. The heatmap will often confirm what branch managers already know; the value is in making that knowledge visible at the portfolio level and in forcing a consistent response.

Building the heatmap matrix that a credit committee can read in ten minutes

The usable output is not a colourful map. It is a matrix that answers three questions:

  1. Which geographies and sectors hold the largest exposure in high or very high physical-risk categories?
  2. What is the change since the last report (new disbursements into high-risk zones, or improvement because of repayments or insurance uptake)?
  3. What is the proposed action—limit freeze, additional collateral, pricing overlay, or enhanced monitoring—and who owns it?

A practical layout for an Indian bank credit committee:

  • Rows: top 15 district-sector combinations by outstanding in high/very high hazard. Group agriculture, MSME, and corporate separately so that the priority-sector concentration is visible.
  • Columns: outstanding (₹ crore), number of accounts, average internal rating, share of total book, share of that sector, insurance coverage percentage, suggested action, owner, target date.
  • A one-page summary that shows the percentage of the total loan book sitting in high/very high physical-risk categories, split by retail, MSME, agriculture, and corporate, and compared with the previous quarter.
  • A short “actions taken” column that records what happened after the last matrix was presented—limits reduced, insurance verified, or monitoring intensified—so the committee can see whether the tool is changing behaviour.

Colour the cells, but keep the numbers. Credit committees read numbers; they use colour only to find the numbers faster. Include a short note on data coverage: “Hazard overlay available for 92 percent of outstanding; remaining 8 percent treated as moderate pending geocoding.” Incomplete coverage stated honestly is more credible than a map that pretends to be complete. For banks that already produce a large-exposure or sector-concentration report, append the physical-risk matrix as an additional schedule rather than creating a separate meeting. The goal is integration, not another committee.

Overlaying transition risk and double-counting discipline

Physical and transition risks interact. A coal-linked borrower in a flood-prone district faces both. A solar project in a high-heat zone may see efficiency losses. The heatmap should therefore carry a second flag for material transition exposure (for example, high Scope 1 intensity, dependence on carbon-intensive inputs, or location in a sector with clear policy phase-down). Do not add the two scores into a single “climate score.” Keep them separate so that the credit committee can see which risk is driving the recommendation.

Double-counting discipline is essential. If the same flood risk has already been reflected in a lower internal rating or in a higher provision, do not apply an additional limit cut. The heatmap is an input to the rating and limit process, not a parallel sanction. Document the interaction rule in the credit policy: “Physical-risk flags inform rating overrides and limit recommendations; they do not automatically trigger both.”

Governance, model risk, and the assurance trail

Because the heatmap will influence credit decisions, it falls under model-risk management. The methodology document should contain:

  • Data sources, update frequency, and known limitations. Explicitly list the IMD, NDMA, CWC, or state sources used and the date of the extract.
  • Scoring rules and any expert overrides, with the name and date of the override. Overrides should be rare and justified (for example, a large corporate that has relocated its plant since the last geocode).
  • Back-testing plan: compare flagged high-risk districts against subsequent NPA or restructuring rates after major events (for example, after a documented flood or drought year). Use the bank’s own NPA data rather than national averages.
  • Ownership: credit risk owns the methodology and the quarterly production; business units supply location data; the sustainability team supplies external hazard updates but does not own the credit output. The chief risk officer or head of credit risk signs the quarterly matrix.
  • Validation frequency: at least annual review of the scoring weights against observed loss experience, and immediate review after any material weather event that affects more than 2 percent of the book.

For assurance, keep an audit trail that shows the input file (loan extract + geocodes), the hazard table used on that date, the scoring script or spreadsheet, and the final matrix presented to the committee. Limited assurance over BRSR climate metrics is becoming more common; a bank that can show the same data lineage for its internal heatmap will be better prepared when supervisors or rating agencies ask how physical risk is managed inside credit. Store the files in the same repository used for other credit-model documentation so that internal audit can sample them without a special request.

Common failure modes in Indian bank practice

Three patterns recur. First, the heatmap is produced once a year by an external consultant and then forgotten until the next request for proposal. The consultant’s map is often national and cannot be refreshed when a new monsoon forecast arrives. Second, geocoding is done only for large corporates while the agricultural and MSME books—which are often the most physically exposed—remain unmapped. In many public-sector and regional banks these two books together exceed 40 percent of advances; leaving them out renders the heatmap unrepresentative. Third, the output is a set of maps without a recommended action or an owner, so the credit committee nods and moves on. A fourth, quieter failure is false precision. A district-level flood map is not an asset-level inundation model. Presenting it as if it were invites both over-reaction and subsequent loss of credibility when the predicted flood does not materialise at every branch. State the resolution clearly and keep the recommended actions proportionate: enhanced monitoring and insurance verification rather than automatic limit cuts on every account in a “high” district.

A fifth failure is the absence of a feedback loop. After a major flood or drought, the bank should compare the heatmap’s high-risk flags with the actual accounts that required restructuring or additional provisioning. If the flags systematically missed the affected borrowers, the vulnerability weights or the geocoding process need revision. If the flags were accurate but no action was taken, the governance process needs revision. Without this loop the heatmap remains a compliance artefact.

Field checklist for the next credit committee pack

  • Loan-book extract geocoded to PIN or better for at least 85 percent of outstanding.
  • Hazard scores drawn from named Indian public sources with the exact vintage and rule documented.
  • Sector and adaptive-capacity vulnerability matrix applied consistently and reviewed by credit.
  • One-page summary plus top-15 matrix with outstanding, share of book, and proposed action/owner.
  • Transition-risk flag kept separate; interaction rule with existing ratings and provisions stated.
  • Data-coverage percentage disclosed; incomplete geocodes treated conservatively.
  • Methodology paper owned by credit risk and lodged with model-risk management.
  • Back-testing plan against the next major weather event already scheduled.
