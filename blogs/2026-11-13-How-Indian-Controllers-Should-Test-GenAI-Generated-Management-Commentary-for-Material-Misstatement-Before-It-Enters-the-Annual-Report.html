Controllers at Indian listed and large unlisted companies are already receiving first drafts of the Management Discussion and Analysis, the Directors’ Report narrative sections, and the BRSR qualitative responses that have been generated or heavily rewritten by a large language model. The productivity gain is real. The control gap is also real. A single unsupported causal claim, an invented competitor comparison, or a quietly altered risk factor can become a material misstatement once the document is signed by the Board and filed with the Registrar and the stock exchanges.

This white paper sets out a practical protocol that Indian controllers can run before any GenAI-assisted commentary leaves the finance function. It is written for the person who still owns the numbers, not for the person who owns the prompt library.

Why GenAI-generated commentary is a material misstatement risk

Management commentary is not marketing copy. Under the Companies Act, 2013 and SEBI (LODR) Regulations, 2015, the Board is responsible for the fair presentation of the company’s affairs. The Directors’ Report and the MD&A form part of the documents on which investors, lenders and regulators place reliance. When those documents contain assertions that are inconsistent with the financial statements, the notes, the risk management disclosures, or the underlying evidence, the inconsistency can be material even if the numbers themselves are correct.

GenAI models are optimised for fluency, not for fidelity to a particular company’s books. They will complete a sentence about “strong pricing power in the southern region” if the prompt mentions southern region and the model has seen similar language elsewhere. They will invent a year-on-year percentage if the prompt asks for “key highlights.” They will soften a going-concern uncertainty into “prudent liquidity management.” None of these behaviours is malicious; all of them create a misstatement risk once the text is adopted without testing.

Controller note: Treat every GenAI-generated sentence that contains a number, a causal claim, a comparison, or a forward-looking statement as untested management information until it has been traced to a source that already exists inside the company’s systems.

The risk is higher in the Indian context for three practical reasons. First, many companies still prepare the narrative sections in the last ten days before the Board meeting; there is little slack for rework. Second, the statutory auditor’s review of the Directors’ Report is limited; the auditor does not re-perform the narrative. Third, SEBI’s enforcement actions on disclosure have focused on both numbers and the accompanying explanation. A fluent but unsupported explanation is not a defence.

What counts as management commentary in Indian reporting practice

For the purpose of this protocol, management commentary includes any narrative that is intended to accompany the financial statements or the BRSR and that will be approved by the Board or a Board committee. The common documents are:

  • The Management Discussion and Analysis section required under Regulation 34 of SEBI LODR.
  • The narrative portions of the Directors’ Report under Section 134 of the Companies Act (business overview, risk factors, internal financial controls, material changes after the balance-sheet date).
  • Qualitative responses in the BRSR and the BRSR Core, especially under Principle 6 (environment) and Principle 8 (inclusive growth) where numbers are explained rather than merely tabulated.
  • Any separate investor presentation or earnings-call script that is later treated as an official disclosure.

A pure product brochure or a careers-page paragraph is outside the protocol. The moment the same language migrates into a Board paper or a filed report, it enters the protocol. Controllers should maintain a short register of every GenAI tool and every prompt family that has been used on any of the documents listed above. The register is not a compliance formality; it is the starting point for the testing work described later.

Three failure modes that convert a draft into a restatement risk

Three patterns account for the majority of material problems that surface when GenAI-assisted commentary is examined after the fact.

Unsupported causal claims

The model writes “margin expansion was driven by the successful implementation of the new procurement system.” The financial statements show margin expansion. The procurement system was implemented, but the controller’s own variance analysis attributes most of the movement to a one-time inventory revaluation and a change in product mix. The causal claim is false. Once published, it becomes a representation that the Board has made about the drivers of performance.

Invented or shifted numbers

The model inserts “EBITDA grew 18 percent” when the management accounts show 11 percent. Or it quotes a market share figure that appeared in a 2023 analyst report but was never verified by the company. Or it rounds a cash conversion cycle from 47 days to “under 40 days.” These are not rounding differences; they are statements of fact that do not exist in the company’s records.

Silent softening of risk language

The original risk factor drafted by the legal and risk teams reads “the company is exposed to a material concentration of revenue with a single public-sector customer whose budget allocation is uncertain.” The GenAI rewrite reads “the company maintains a diversified customer base while remaining attentive to public-sector opportunities.” The concentration has disappeared. The Board has not been asked to approve the change; the change has simply arrived in a cleaner draft.

Emerging-economy practicality: In companies where the finance team is small and the same person prepares both the notes and the MD&A, the temptation to accept a fluent rewrite is highest. The protocol below is deliberately short enough that a two-person finance team can complete it without hiring an external reviewer.

A five-step validation protocol that fits inside two working days

The protocol assumes the controller already has a near-final set of financial statements and a draft commentary that has been generated or heavily edited by GenAI. It does not require new software. It requires a marked-up copy, a source file, and one meeting.

  1. Extract every factual assertion. Highlight every sentence that contains a number, a percentage, a ranking, a causal verb (“driven by,” “resulted in,” “enabled”), a comparison with prior periods or peers, or a forward-looking statement. Do not highlight pure qualitative adjectives that cannot be tested. A typical MD&A yields 40–80 such assertions. Number them.
  2. Map each assertion to an existing internal source. Acceptable sources are the signed financial statements, the management accounts already reviewed by the audit committee, the variance analysis pack, the risk register approved by the Board, and any external data the company has previously cited with a source. “The model said so” is not a source. If no source exists, the assertion is deleted or rewritten as an unquantified management view.
  3. Test consistency with the notes and the risk factors. Read the assertion against the relevant note and against the principal risk factors. If the note discloses a concentration and the commentary claims diversification, one of them must change. The financial statements and the risk factors have already been through a more rigorous process; they win.
  4. Run a “materiality read” by a second person. A second member of the finance team (or the company secretary if the finance team is single-handed) reads only the highlighted assertions and asks: “If this sentence is wrong, would a reasonable investor or lender care?” Assertions that survive this filter are traced; the rest can be left as colour.
  5. Lock the tested version. The controller initials a cover note that lists the number of assertions tested, the number deleted or rewritten, and the location of the source file. That cover note travels with the Board paper. No further GenAI editing is permitted after the lock.

The entire sequence can be completed in two working days if the financial statements are already closed. Day 1 is extraction and mapping. Day 2 is the consistency check, the second-person read, and the lock. Companies that wait until the night before the Board meeting will not finish; the protocol therefore belongs in the annual-report timetable two weeks before the audit-committee meeting.

How to document the protocol so internal audit and the statutory auditor accept it

Internal audit and the statutory auditor will not re-perform the narrative. They will, however, ask whether the company has a process for ensuring that the commentary is consistent with the financial statements. A one-page process note plus the assertion log is sufficient evidence.

The process note should state:

  • Which documents are in scope.
  • Which GenAI tools are approved for use on those documents (and that personal accounts are prohibited).
  • The five steps above, with the name of the person who owns each step.
  • The retention period for the assertion log and the source file (recommend seven years, aligned with the company’s document-retention policy).

The assertion log itself can be a simple spreadsheet: assertion number, sentence, source document and page or cell reference, second-person initials, disposition (retained / rewritten / deleted). When the statutory auditor asks for evidence of management’s review of the Directors’ Report, the controller produces the log and the cover note. That is usually the end of the inquiry.

Board and audit committee questions that force the protocol to exist

Most Boards will not spontaneously demand a GenAI validation protocol. The audit committee chair, or the independent director who sits on the risk committee, can force the issue with three questions that take less than five minutes:

  1. “Which sections of the Directors’ Report and the MD&A were drafted or rewritten with the assistance of a large language model, and where is the log of assertions that were traced to source?”
  2. “If a sentence in the MD&A is inconsistent with a note to the accounts, which document prevails, and who has the authority to change the sentence after the financial statements have been signed off by the auditors?”
  3. “Has the company prohibited the use of personal GenAI accounts for any document that will be filed with the stock exchanges or the Ministry of Corporate Affairs?”

A controller who can answer all three with a file reference has already built the protocol. A controller who cannot answer them has a gap that will be visible the first time a disclosure query arrives from an exchange or a rating agency.

Board practicality: Place the three questions on the audit-committee agenda as a standing item for the meeting that considers the annual report. They require no new resolution; they simply make the existing responsibility for fair presentation operational.

Closing checklist for the next annual report cycle

Controllers can use the following checklist in the fortnight before the audit-committee meeting that considers the annual report. Each item is binary; partial completion is treated as incomplete.

  • All GenAI-assisted commentary documents have been identified and listed.
  • Every factual assertion has been extracted and numbered.
  • Each assertion has been mapped to an existing internal source or has been deleted or rewritten as an unquantified view.
  • Consistency with the notes to the accounts and the principal risk factors has been checked by the controller.
  • A second person has performed the materiality read and initialled the log.
  • The tested version has been locked; no further GenAI editing is permitted.
  • The one-page process note and the assertion log are included in the Board paper pack.
  • Personal GenAI accounts are confirmed as prohibited for any filed document.
  • The annual-report timetable for the following year already contains the two-day validation window.

The protocol does not eliminate the use of GenAI. It simply restores the same standard of evidence that the controller already applies to every number that appears in the financial statements. Fluency is not a substitute for that standard. Once the assertion log exists, the productivity gain can be kept and the material-misstatement risk can be reduced to a level the Board can consciously accept.
