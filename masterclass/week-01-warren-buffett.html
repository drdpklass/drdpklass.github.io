Top Investor Learning System · Week 01 · 19 Sep 2026

Warren Buffett
Capital allocation as a craft

Not a biography. A working reconstruction of how Buffett selected businesses, priced them, concentrated capital, used insurance float, and changed his mind when the evidence changed. Primary sources: Berkshire owner’s letters (1977–2024), partnership letters (1957–1969), and annual reports. Interpretation is labelled as such.

1. Life timeline2. Investment evolution3. Major investments4. Mental models5. Primary sources6. What he got wrong7. Investor playbook8. Modern application9. Case study10. Implementation11. Scorecard12. Cheat sheet13. Reading

1. Life timeline

1930–1942

Omaha childhood and first commerce

Born 30 Aug 1930. Father Howard Buffett was a stockbroker and later a U.S. Congressman. Early businesses: paper routes, pinball machines. The habit that later matters is unit economics at street level.

1942–1951

First stock, then Graham at Columbia

Rejected by Harvard Business School. Enrolled at Columbia because Benjamin Graham and David Dodd taught there. Read The Intelligent Investor and Security Analysis as operating manuals.

1951–1956

Graham-Newman apprenticeship

Net-nets: buy below net current asset value. Graham retired; Buffett returned to Omaha.

1956–1969

Buffett Partnership Ltd.

Three buckets in the letters: generals, workouts, controls. Compounded about 29.5% annually before fees vs high-single-digit Dow (partnership letters). Closed the partnership because bargains disappeared.

1965–1985

Berkshire textile mill to holding company

Control in 1965. Textile economics were poor. Bought National Indemnity (1967) and See’s (1972). Insurance float became the silent partner.

1978–2011

Munger partnership of mind

Shift from cheap-and-mediocre to wonderful-at-a-fair-price. Coca-Cola 1988; BNSF 2010.

2016–2025

Apple, cash, succession

Apple became the largest common-stock holding. Greg Abel designated successor for operations. Letters state that size now caps percentage returns.

2. Investment evolution

Early doctrine (Graham)

Statistical cheapness. Margin of safety is a discount to liquidation value. Quality is secondary if price is low enough.

What changed

See’s showed pricing power compounds better than a cheap mill. 1970s inflation punished capital-hungry mediocre businesses. Munger pushed quality.

What stayed constant

Owner orientation. Circle of competence. No market forecasts. Cash earnings over reported earnings when they diverge. Cheap understood liabilities only (float), not margin debt.

Late-career constraint

Scale. The philosophy did not reverse; the opportunity set shrank to mega-caps and whole companies.

1989 letter (Munger formulation): better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.

3. Major investments (8)

1. Berkshire textiles (1962–85)

Cigar-butt cheapness in a structurally dying industry. He later called it his worst purchase because of opportunity cost. Lesson: cheap assets in a shrinking industry are not a bargain.

2. National Indemnity (1967)

An insurer that walked away from underpriced risk. If combined ratio stays at or under 100 over a cycle, float is better than a zero-coupon loan. Template for later insurance capital.

3. See’s Candies (1972)

About $25m for a brand with pricing power and low incremental capital. Teaching case for economic goodwill. Pay up only when incremental capital earns high returns.

4. Washington Post (1973)

Dominant franchise in the 1973–74 crash. Multi-bagger. Later the newspaper moat decayed. Crash-buying of quality worked; media moats are not permanent.

5. Coca-Cola (1988+)

Global brand, tiny capital needs vs earnings. Paid ~15x then-earnings — not a cigar butt. Durability of incremental ROIC was the justification.

6. American Express (1964 salad-oil; later core)

Temporary hit to a franchise whose reputation was intact. Distinguish a hole in reported book from a hole in the franchise.

7. BNSF (2010, ~$44bn including debt)

Irreplaceable rails, replacement cost above market, fuel efficiency vs truck. Forever infrastructure with acceptable reinvestment returns.

8. Apple (2016+)

Framed by him as a consumer franchise with switching costs and buybacks, not as a chip cycle. Largest recent equity contributor. Test of whether the circle of competence can expand without becoming a slogan.

4. Mental models and principles

Circle of competence

Define the edge as what you can value. Size of the circle matters less than knowing the rim.

Mr. Market

Use the quote when useful; never let it set intrinsic value.

Margin of safety

Conservative assumptions must still work. Safety is in price vs owner earnings, not in diversification theatre.

Owner earnings (1986 letter)

Reported earnings + D&A − maintenance capex ± working-capital reality.

Float as cheap leverage

Only if underwriting is profitable. Bad underwriting is hidden debt.

Opportunity-cost hurdle

Rarely a textbook WACC. Can I do better with the next idea I understand?

Incentives

Read pay, volume targets, and whether managers are paid to grow premium or underwriting profit.

Institutional imperative (1989)

Firms imitate peers and spend surplus because they have it. Fight it in yourself first.

  • Berkshire Chairman’s letters 1977–2024 (berkshirehathaway.com).
  • Partnership letters 1957–1969.
  • 1986 letter: owner-earnings formula.
  • 1989 letter: institutional imperative; wonderful vs fair price.
  • 2010 materials on BNSF.

Purchase prices and letter text are facts. “Apple was bought as a consumer franchise” is a reasoned inference from later wording, not a 2016 internal memo we possess.

6. What they got wrong

  • Textiles: self-described error; opportunity cost lasted years.
  • Dexter Shoe (1993): paid in Berkshire shares for a business destroyed by imports.
  • USAir preferred (1989): violated his own industry-quality filter.
  • Salomon 1991: franchise plus culture risk.
  • IBM 2011–18: cloud transition faster than the model.
  • Missing Google/Amazon early: circle-of-competence has a cost.
  • Size drag: he states that 20%+ forever is no longer available at Berkshire scale.

7. Investor playbook

Select

Understandable product, durable demand, high incremental ROIC, pricing power, honest owners.

Value

Owner earnings, defendable growth, opportunity-cost discount rate. No false precision.

Management

Judge uses of cash: buybacks only below value, dividends if the firm cannot reinvest, no empire building.

Moat

Brand that prices, switching costs, regulated infrastructure, low-cost producer, true network effects.

Debt

Avoid firms that need leverage to produce acceptable equity returns. No personal margin on securities.

Concentration

When the edge is real, weight it. Diversify when you do not know.

Crashes

Cash is a call option on forced sellers. Work is done before the crash: a list you already understand.

Buy / hold / sell

Buy when price is well below conservative value. Hold while moat and allocation remain. Sell when the thesis breaks or a clearly better use appears.

8. Modern application

Still transfers

  • Owner earnings vs GAAP theatre.
  • Incentives inside promoter companies.
  • Crash lists + cash.
  • Saying no as AUM or ego grows.

Does not transfer cleanly

  • Newspaper oligopolies are gone.
  • Do not imitate float with personal leverage.
  • Lease capitalisation and SBC need extra adjustments.
  • Passive flows can keep Mr. Market wrong longer.

9. Case study

Assignment: Asian Paints through a Buffett lens

Write the memo before opening the model answer. Questions: circle of competence (dealers, TiO2, housing cycle); moat vs past ROE; owner earnings vs growth capex; surplus-cash uses; thesis killers; buy yield vs your hurdle.

Model solution (open after you write yours)

Pass if you cannot explain dealer economics and tinting-machine lock-in in one paragraph. Historical high ROCE is evidence of something; durable pieces are brand in a trust good and installed tinting/dealer WC. Weakening pieces: organised competition and project mix. Start from PAT, add D&A, subtract maintenance capex; treat large capacity as growth capex. Promoter-led conservative balance sheet is a plus; unrelated diversification is institutional-imperative risk. Buy only when mid-cycle owner-earnings yield clears your hurdle — not on peak housing margins. Sell if dealer economics commoditise or capital repeatedly goes to low-ROIC adjacencies.

10. Implementation challenge

  1. Read Berkshire 1986 or 1989 or 2014 end-to-end. Write 10 lines: useful vs dated.
  2. Build a 3-year owner-earnings worksheet for one listed company. Separate maintenance vs growth capex in writing.
  3. Pre-mortem: “It is 2029 and this investment failed because…” Three specific causes become your sell checklist.

11. Scorecard (your learning)

Score yourself 0–5 after the exercises. Do not score Buffett.

Business analysis · Accounting (PAT to owner earnings) · Valuation as owner-earnings yield · Operational thesis-killers · Capital allocation by uses of cash · Behaviour (wrote before opening the answer) · Independence (disagreed with one popular narrative and said why).

Core philosophyBuy understandable businesses with durable earning power at a discount to conservative owner value; let retained earnings work.

Favourite questionsHow does it make cash? What stops a rival? What does this firm look like in 10 years with a fool in charge? What is my next-best use of this capital?
