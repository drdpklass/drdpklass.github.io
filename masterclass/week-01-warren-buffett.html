Top Investor Learning System · Edition 01

Warren Buffett

Not a biography. A working reconstruction of how Buffett selected businesses, priced them, used insurance float, concentrated when odds were extreme, and documented his own errors in the Berkshire letters. Primary source of record: Berkshire Hathaway chairman’s letters and partnership letters.

1. Life timeline (decision-relevant)

PeriodWhat actually formed the investor
1930–1949Omaha; early trading and small businesses. The habit that survives is counting cash.
1950–51Columbia; Benjamin Graham. GEICO visit (1951) is the first documented quality + distribution-advantage observation.
1954–56Graham-Newman. Cigar-butt liquidation arithmetic and buying below net working capital.
1956–69Buffett Partnership Ltd: work-outs, generally undervalued securities, control situations. Closed 1969 after bargains disappeared.
1965Control of Berkshire. Later letters call the textile purchase a capital-allocation error.
1972See’s Candies: hinge from cheap assets to durable earning power with pricing power.
1976–96GEICO rescue and full ownership. Insurance float becomes the structural edge.
1988–94Coca-Cola accumulation. Wonderful company at a fair price.
2009–10BNSF: understandable, regulated utility-like cash flows at crisis pricing.
2016–18Apple becomes the largest equity holding: brand, switching costs, capital return — not a generic tech call.
2020sCash, buybacks below estimated intrinsic value, public error catalogue (EFH, Dexter, some bank/airline timing).

2. Investment evolution

Early (Graham)

Statistically cheap securities, net-nets, special situations. Holding period can be short if the gap closes.

What changed

See’s (1972) and Munger. A business that can raise prices without losing volume compounds owner earnings. The 1989 letter publishes the rule already practiced in the 1970s: wonderful company at a fair price beats a fair company at a wonderful price.

What did not change

  • Stocks are pieces of businesses.
  • Margin of safety; quality only changes where the margin lives.
  • Circle of competence.
  • Per-share intrinsic value, not empire size.
  • Insurance float as cheap leverage rather than bank debt.

3. Major investments (8)

GEICO (1951; 1976 rescue; 1995–96 full buy)

Saw: direct-writer cost advantage. Others in 1976: treated a reserving crisis as a dead franchise. Risk: growth that outruns capital. Today: a crisis in a low-cost producer is not the same as a crisis in a high-cost producer.

See’s Candies (1972)

About $25m for a business earning roughly $4–5m pre-tax. Pricing power, brand, tiny incremental capital. Template of a “great” business in the 2007 letter.

The Washington Post (1973)

Bear-market purchase of a dominant local franchise at a fraction of conservative intrinsic value. Catalyst: time plus cash generation.

Coca-Cola (1988–94)

Global brand, bottler system, pricing, high incremental returns. Paid a “high” multiple versus cigar-butt history. Risk was valuation and volume, not insolvency.

Newspapers / Buffalo News

Local monopoly economics that later letters admit the internet destroyed. The model was era-correct; the moat was not perpetual.

BNSF (2010, ~$44bn including debt)

Irreplaceable rails, replacement cost above purchase price, long-haul advantage versus trucks. Capital-heavy is acceptable if incremental returns and barriers are high.

Bank of America (2011 preferred + warrants)

6% preferred plus warrants when the bank needed a confidence signal. Asymmetry written into the contract.

Apple (from 2016)

Consumer franchise, switching costs, services attach, buybacks that raise per-share ownership. Fits See’s/Coke more than a tech conversion.

4. Mental models and principles

Owner earnings

1986 letter: reported earnings adjusted for non-cash items minus maintenance capex.

Circle of competence

The edge is knowing the boundary. Avoidance is a position.

Mr. Market

Graham’s servant. Price is data, not a thesis.

Float

Cheap leverage only if reserving is honest.

Opportunity cost

Every yes is a no to the next idea.

Institutional imperative

1989 letter: resist imitation and expansion for its own sake.

6. What he got wrong

  • Berkshire textiles (1965): cheap stock, dying economics.
  • Dexter Shoe (1993) paid in undervalued Berkshire stock — double error.
  • USAir preferred (1989): airline cost structure miss.
  • Energy Future Holdings debt: named ~$873m write-down in 2011.
  • IBM (2011–18): fading services franchise treated as a durable coupon.
  • Airlines 2020: bought then sold four majors.
  • Newspaper/retail moat decay acknowledged after the fact.

7. Investor playbook

QuestionBuffett rule
SelectSimple, durable, understandable 10-year economics, able honest managers, sensible price.
ValueDiscount owner earnings. Do not pay for growth you cannot see.
ManagementCapital allocation + integrity. Watch compensation and share issuance.
MoatPricing power, low-cost production, barriers. Can a well-funded rival kill it in 10 years?
DebtAvoid operating leverage stacked on financial leverage. Prefer float.
ConcentrationFew bets when odds are extreme.
CrashesBuy more of understood businesses. Do not sell because the quote is red.
Buy/hold/sellBuy below conservative intrinsic value. Hold while moat and allocation hold. Sell if thesis breaks or a far better use of capital appears.

8. Modern application

Still transfers

Owner earnings, competence boundaries, opportunity cost, distrust of advertised EBITDA, per-share thinking, crash temperament.

Does not transfer blindly

1970s newspaper economics; brand + TV ads as a sufficient moat; assuming US insurance float is available to a small Indian partnership; treating every consumer app as See’s. Modern accounting (leases, SBC, capitalized software) hides more than 1986 statements.

9. Case study — you analyse first

Company: Asian Paints (India)

Write a one-page Buffett memo before opening the solution.

  1. Circle of competence: dealer network, tinting machines, working capital, input pass-through.
  2. Moat versus Berger/Kansai; housing-cycle threat.
  3. Owner earnings: PAT + D&A − conservative maintenance/growth capex. Incremental ROIC on depots.
  4. Capital allocation: dividends, buybacks, capacity, promoter behaviour.
  5. Implied owner-earnings yield versus 1988 Coke multiple.
  6. What would make you pass even if the business is wonderful?
Model solution (open after you write)

Asian Paints is a See’s/Coke-type franchise: brand + dealer density + tinting installed base. Incremental depot capital historically earned high returns. The usual fail is price, not quality. A wonderful paint company at 70–80× owner earnings fails the Coke test unless year-10 owner earnings are defensible without heroic share gains. Quality is necessary, not sufficient.

10. Implementation challenge (this week)

  1. Read Berkshire 1989 or 2007 end-to-end. Write 10 lines: one principle, one number, one named mistake, one rule you will use.
  2. Compute conservative owner earnings for one company. State maintenance capex as an explicit assumption.
  3. One-page journal: “What would make me sell this?” for your largest holding. If the answer is a lower quote, rewrite it.

12. One-page cheat sheet

Investor

Warren Buffett — allocator through Berkshire + float.

Core philosophy

Buy pieces of understandable businesses below conservative owner-earnings value.

Key models

Mr. Market · margin of safety · circle of competence · owner earnings · opportunity cost · institutional imperative · float.

Favourite questions

Would I buy the whole company? How much cash over a decade? What kills the moat? What is management doing with retained earnings?

Valuation

Owner earnings discounted; no undefended growth; price vs per-share intrinsic value.

Risk rules

Avoid permanent capital loss; distrust leverage; stay inside competence; cash is an option.

Biggest documented mistake

Dexter Shoe paid in Berkshire stock — plus the original textile control purchase.

5 rules

Business first. Write owner earnings. Pay for durability. Name errors. Inactivity is a decision.

Cumulative learning system

Week 01 is the base layer: businesses, owner earnings, competence, documented humility. Week 02 (Munger) adds latticework models, inversion, and incentives — the pressure that moved Buffett off cigar butts. Carry forward: price is not value.

Sequence: Buffett → Munger → Graham → Fisher → Lynch → Templeton → Marks → Klarman → Greenblatt → Pabrai → Li Lu → Nick Sleep → Jhunjhunwala.
