Module 1: Digital Payments and Modern Lending

Curated by Dr. Debasis Pahi · Assistant Professor (OES-I), Model Degree College Malkangiri · Ph.D. (IIT Kharagpur)
3 lessons · Hands-on Google Sheets lab · Free study guide

Mentor note: You do not need a banking degree to understand this. Think of money movement the way you think of sending a WhatsApp message — the “message” is value, and the rails are the systems that carry it safely.

LESSON 1

How UPI, Digital Wallets, and Cross-Border Payments Work Under the Hood

Imagine you and a friend sit in different rooms of a house. You want to give your friend ₹100. In the old world you would walk over with cash. In the digital world you need a trusted messenger who both rooms already trust.

Analogy: UPI is that messenger for India — a shared, regulated system both banks trust.

When you scan a QR code or enter a UPI ID, your app does three quick things:

  1. Identity check – “Is this really the account holder?” (PIN / biometrics).
  2. Balance & limit check – your bank confirms you have the money.
  3. Settlement instruction – NPCI tells the two banks to update their ledgers. Money does not physically travel; two entries are updated almost instantly.

Digital wallets (Paytm balance, PhonePe wallet, Amazon Pay, etc.) work like a prepaid lunch card. You load money once; later you spend from that balance without touching your bank every time. The wallet company holds the float and settles with merchants in bulk.

Cross-border payments are slower because two countries’ systems must talk. Traditionally this meant a chain of correspondent banks (like posting a letter through three post offices). Newer rails — UPI international, specialised FinTech corridors, and CBDC experiments — try to shorten that chain so a remittance feels closer to a domestic transfer.

Everyday takeaway: UPI = instant domestic messenger · Wallet = prepaid card · Cross-border = still partly “air mail,” but getting faster.
LESSON 2

Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Lending and Alternative Credit Scoring

Traditional banks ask for salary slips, IT returns and a credit bureau score. Many good borrowers (students, freelancers, small shop owners) fail that test even if they repay on time in real life.

P2P lending platforms connect people who have surplus money (lenders) with people who need money (borrowers). The platform is the marketplace and the referee — it does not usually use its own balance sheet the way a bank does.

How do they decide whom to lend to without a classic bank file?

They use alternative data:

  • Mobile recharge and bill-payment history
  • E-commerce and UPI transaction patterns
  • Device and app behaviour (stable device, consistent location)
  • Cash-flow from digital sales for small businesses
  • Other permitted behavioural or professional signals (within privacy rules)
Analogy: Like a college admission office that looks beyond board marks — attendance, projects, recommendations — the model outputs a risk score. The platform sets an interest rate and often splits one loan across many small lenders so no single person bears the whole risk.

Risks to remember: Higher rates than prime bank loans, possible delays in recovery, and the need for clear disclosure. Good platforms show you the full cost and collection process upfront.

Everyday takeaway: P2P + alternative scoring = lending based on digital behaviour, not only paper documents.
LESSON 3

Open Banking and APIs — How Financial Data Is Securely Shared

You already share data when you allow a food app to use your location. Open Banking is the regulated version of that idea for money.

Analogy: An API (Application Programming Interface) is a waiter in a restaurant. You do not walk into the kitchen; you tell the waiter what you want; the waiter brings it according to the kitchen’s rules. In finance, your bank is the kitchen. A budgeting or loan app is the customer. The API is the waiter that fetches balance or initiates a payment only after you give explicit consent.

Key safeguards:

  • You must opt in (consent), and you can revoke it.
  • Data is usually shared for a limited purpose and time.
  • Strong authentication (often the same UPI/PIN-style checks).
  • Regulators set rules around account aggregation and data protection.

Result: a loan app can see your bank cash-flow (with permission) and decide faster; you can pull all accounts into one dashboard without giving every fintech your password.

Everyday takeaway: Open Banking = controlled data sharing via APIs, with you holding the consent switch.
HANDS-ON LAB

Build a Simple P2P Loan Amortization Schedule in Google Sheets

Simulate a ₹50,000 loan for 12 months at 14% annual interest (a common ballpark for some digital personal loans).

Step-by-step

  1. Open Google Sheets → Blank spreadsheet.
  2. Enter labels and inputs:
    A1: Loan Amount   B1: 50000
    A2: Annual Rate   B2: 0.14
    A3: Tenure (months)   B3: 12
  3. Monthly rate:
    A4: Monthly Rate   B4: =B2/12
  4. EMI (standard amortization):
    A5: EMI   B5: =PMT(B4,B3,-B1)
    You should see a monthly EMI around ₹4,490.
  5. Schedule headers in row 7:
    Month | Opening Balance | EMI | Interest | Principal | Closing Balance
  6. Month 1 formulas:
    A8: 1
    B8: =B1
    C8: =$B$5
    D8: =B8*$B$4
    E8: =C8-D8
    F8: =B8-E8
  7. Month 2:
    A9: =A8+1   B9: =F8   C9: =$B$5
    D9: =B9*$B$4   E9: =C9-D9   F9: =B9-E9
  8. Drag row 9 formulas down through row 19 (12 months).
  9. Check that the final closing balance is ~0 (small rounding differences are normal).
  10. Optional: Cumulative Interest column with =SUM($D$8:D8) and drag down — this shows total interest paid over time.

What you just practised: Every EMI splits into interest (cost of money) and principal (debt reduction). Early months are interest-heavy; later months clear principal faster. That is the core of how P2P and digital lenders structure repayments.

Module 2: Blockchain and the Future of Trust

Curated by Dr. Debasis Pahi · Assistant Professor (OES-I), Model Degree College Malkangiri · Ph.D. (IIT Kharagpur)
3 conceptual lessons · Free testnet lab (no real money, no coding degree required)

Educator note: Blockchain is not magic and you do not need cryptography maths to grasp it. Think of it as a shared notebook that many people hold copies of — once a page is written and agreed, changing it without everyone noticing is extremely hard.

LESSON 1

What Is a Blockchain Ledger and How Does It Prevent Tampering?

A ledger is simply a record of transactions — who paid whom, how much, and when. Banks keep private ledgers. A blockchain is a ledger that is:

  • Shared – many computers hold a copy
  • Append-only – new pages (blocks) are added; old pages are not quietly rewritten
  • Linked – each block contains a fingerprint of the previous block
Analogy: Imagine a class notebook. Every student has a photocopy. When a new page is written, everyone updates their copy. If someone tries to change page 5 in their book alone, the fingerprint on page 6 will no longer match everyone else’s. The class rejects the fake version.

That “fingerprint” idea (a hash) is how tampering is detected. You do not need to calculate hashes yourself — just remember: change one past entry and the chain of fingerprints breaks. Networks agree on the true chain through consensus rules (for example, majority of honest participants or economic incentives). That is why public blockchains are described as tamper-resistant, not “impossible to attack,” but extremely expensive and visible to attack.

Everyday takeaway: Blockchain = shared notebook with linked pages. Tampering breaks the chain that everyone else still holds.
LESSON 2

Smart Contracts: What Are They and How Do They Automate Agreements?

A normal contract is a PDF or paper that lawyers and courts interpret. A smart contract is a small program stored on a blockchain that runs exactly as written when conditions are met.

Analogy: A vending machine. You insert the right coins and press the button; the machine releases the snack. No cashier decides “maybe later.” The rules are built into the machine. Smart contracts are vending machines for digital agreements — escrow, insurance payouts, simple swaps, loyalty points.

Typical flow:

  1. Parties agree on rules (e.g., “If shipment is confirmed, release payment”).
  2. Rules are written as code and deployed to a blockchain.
  3. When data or payments arrive that satisfy the rules, the contract executes automatically.
  4. Results are recorded on the ledger for all relevant parties to verify.

Important limits: Code does what it is told — bugs and unfair rules still hurt people. Oracles (data feeds from the outside world) must be trustworthy. Smart contracts do not replace law; they automate clear, digital conditions.

Everyday takeaway: Smart contract = vending machine for agreements. Same input rules → same automatic output, recorded on the shared ledger.
LESSON 3

Central Bank Digital Currencies (CBDCs) vs. Cryptocurrencies

Both can be digital, but they answer different questions of who issues money and who controls the rules.

Aspect Cryptocurrency (e.g. Bitcoin) CBDC
Issuer Decentralised network / protocol Central bank of a country
Legal status Often not legal tender Digital form of national currency (legal tender by design)
Purpose Open, borderless value transfer; store of value experiments Efficient payments, inclusion, monetary policy tools
Control & privacy Varies; public chains are transparent by default Designed by the state; privacy vs oversight is a policy choice
Analogy: Cryptocurrency is like a community token used in a large online game — useful inside that ecosystem, rules set by the protocol. A CBDC is like digital cash issued by the same authority that issues paper notes — still “rupees,” just in a new form, with national policy goals.

Many countries are researching or piloting CBDCs. Design choices (retail vs wholesale, online vs offline capability, holding limits) matter as much as the technology.

Everyday takeaway: Crypto ≈ open network money experiments. CBDC ≈ state-issued digital cash. Same “digital,” different issuer, trust model and purpose.
HANDS-ON LAB (FREE · NO REAL MONEY)

Deploy a Simulated Smart Contract with Remix IDE (Beginner Path)

Goal: See a tiny “agreement” live on a test network — fake coins only. No coding degree required. You will copy a ready-made sample, compile it, and deploy it in the browser.

Safety first

  • Use only test networks (Sepolia, Remix VM). Never paste a real seed phrase or private key from a wallet that holds real money.
  • This lab uses Remix’s built-in simulator or a public testnet faucet — both are free.
  • If a site asks you to “connect wallet and approve unlimited spending,” stop. You do not need that for this exercise.

Option A — Easiest (everything inside the browser, zero wallet setup)

  1. Open https://remix.ethereum.org in Chrome or Firefox.
  2. In the left file explorer, create a new file: SimpleStore.sol.
  3. Delete any default code and paste this entire sample (it only stores a number — like a shared notice board): 
    // SPDX-License-Identifier: MIT
pragma solidity ^0.8.0;

contract SimpleStore {
    uint public value;
    address public lastUpdater;

    function set(uint newValue) public {
        value = newValue;
        lastUpdater = msg.sender;
    }
}
  4. Open the Solidity Compiler tab (left icons). Choose compiler 0.8.x. Click Compile SimpleStore.sol. Wait for a green check.
  5. Open the Deploy & Run tab. Environment: select Remix VM (Shanghai) or similar simulated chain — this uses fake accounts with free test ETH.
  6. Click Deploy. Under “Deployed Contracts,” expand SimpleStore.
  7. In the set field type a number (e.g. 42) → click set. Then click value — you should see 42. Click lastUpdater to see which simulated address wrote it.
  8. You just “deployed” and used a smart contract. The state lives on the simulated ledger until you refresh.

Option B — Real testnet (still free, optional)

  1. Install a browser wallet (e.g. MetaMask). Create a new account used only for learning.
  2. In MetaMask, add/switch to Sepolia test network.
  3. Get free Sepolia ETH from a public faucet (search “Sepolia faucet”; complete any simple captcha). Never send mainnet ETH to a test address.
  4. In Remix → Deploy & Run → Environment: Injected Provider – MetaMask. Deploy the same contract. Confirm the test transaction in MetaMask (fee is test ETH only).
  5. Call set / value as before. You can look up the contract address on a Sepolia block explorer.

What you practised: A smart contract is just rules + stored state on a ledger. Deploy → call a function → read the updated state. On a testnet you learn the flow without risking real funds.

Module 3: Smart Insurance & Risk Technology

Curated by Dr. Debasis Pahi · Assistant Professor (OES-I), Model Degree College Malkangiri · Ph.D. (IIT Kharagpur)
3 lessons · DIY flowchart lab (Miro or Draw.io · free)

Innovator note: Insurance is moving from “file a claim and wait” to “sense the risk and settle faster.” You do not need an actuarial degree — only a clear picture of data, triggers and fairness.

LESSON 1

Telematics and Usage-Based Insurance (Pay-How-You-Drive)

Traditional car insurance often prices you by age, location and past claims — not by how carefully you drive this month. Telematics changes that.

Analogy: A fitness band for your car (or your phone acting as one). It notices hard braking, late-night trips, average speed and smooth cornering — then the insurer can reward safer patterns with a lower premium.

How it works in practice:

  1. You opt in to a usage-based policy.
  2. A small plug-in device, a dedicated app, or phone sensors collect driving signals (with your consent).
  3. The platform turns those signals into a risk score (e.g. smooth braking = good; frequent harsh stops = higher risk).
  4. Premium is adjusted — often monthly — based on actual behaviour, not only a yearly snapshot.

Why insurers like it: Better risk selection and incentives for safer roads. Why customers care: Careful drivers can pay less; occasional drivers may prefer “pay-per-mile” variants. Watch-outs: Privacy, consent, and clear rules on what data is stored and for how long.

Everyday takeaway: Usage-based insurance = premium linked to measured behaviour (how and how much you drive), not only static profile factors.
LESSON 2

Parametric Insurance — Automatic Payouts from Weather (and Other) Data

In classic crop insurance, a farmer reports a loss, an inspector may visit, and payment can take weeks. Parametric insurance flips the process: payout is tied to a measurable index, not a lengthy damage survey.

Analogy: A rain gauge connected to an automatic locker. If rainfall in your district falls below an agreed threshold during the sowing window, the locker opens and the agreed sum is released — no debate about “how dry was my field exactly.”

Typical crop parametric flow:

  • Index: rainfall, temperature, wind speed, satellite vegetation index, etc., from trusted weather/satellite sources.
  • Trigger: e.g. “rainfall < X mm over Y days in district Z.”
  • Payout rule: fixed amount or a simple scale (mild / severe shortfall).
  • Settlement: when the index is confirmed, payment is released to enrolled farmers — often via bank or mobile money.

Strength: speed and lower admin cost. Limitation (basis risk): the index might not match every individual farm’s loss perfectly. Good design uses local data, clear communication and sometimes a hybrid with traditional cover.

Everyday takeaway: Parametric = “if the public number crosses the line, pay.” Fast, transparent, best when the index closely tracks real harm.
LESSON 3

AI in Claims Processing — Photos That Help Assess Damage

After a minor car accident or a flooded room, customers often wait for an assessor. AI-assisted claims use photos (and sometimes video) to estimate damage faster.

Analogy: A trained assistant who has seen thousands of dented bumpers and water-stained ceilings. You upload clear photos; the assistant suggests repair type and rough cost; a human still reviews edge cases and fraud signals.

Typical journey:

  1. Customer reports a claim in the app and uploads photos from specified angles.
  2. Computer vision models detect damaged parts, severity bands and possible total-loss flags.
  3. The system proposes a settlement range or garage pathway.
  4. Straight-through processing for simple, low-value claims; human experts for complex or suspicious cases.

Benefits: shorter wait times, lower inspection cost, more consistent first estimates. Responsibilities: model bias, photo quality, fraud (staged images), and always a path to human review. AI should speed fairness — not replace accountability.

Everyday takeaway: AI claims = photos + models for a first estimate; humans still own exceptions, fairness and final trust.

DIY: Flowchart a Fictional Automated Crop Insurance Platform

Design the journey of a parametric crop product — from farmer enrolment to automatic payout — using a free diagram tool.

Tools (pick one)

  • Miro — free plan · infinite canvas · flowchart shapes
  • Draw.io (diagrams.net) — free · runs in browser · no sign-up required for basic use

What to draw (minimum viable flow)

  1. Enrolment: Farmer signs up (name, land location/district, crop, acreage, mobile/bank details) → KYC / eligibility check → policy confirmation SMS.
  2. Cover period: Season start → weather/satellite data feeds update the index daily.
  3. Trigger engine: Compare index to threshold → if trigger met, create payout instruction; if not, continue monitoring until season end.
  4. Payout: Notify farmer → push payment via bank / UPI / mobile money → record on ledger for audit.
  5. Exceptions: Data gap, disputed district boundary, or farmer query → route to human support queue.
  6. Close: Season ends, no trigger → send “cover completed” message; store anonymised stats for next season pricing.

Suggested boxes to include

Use rectangles for steps, diamonds for decisions (e.g. “Rainfall < threshold?”), and arrows for flow. Label data sources: “IMD / satellite API,” “Consent store,” “Payments API.”

Reflection (3 lines on your diagram or in a note)

  • Where could basis risk hurt a farmer?
  • Where must a human still decide?
  • What consent message would you show at enrolment?

Optional stretch: add a second swimlane for “Insurer / regulator audit” showing how every automatic payout remains explainable.

Module 4: Making Decisions with Financial Data

Curated by Dr. Debasis Pahi · Assistant Professor (OES-I), Model Degree College Malkangiri · Ph.D. (IIT Kharagpur)
Data literacy · Free tools only (Google Sheets + Google Colab) · Copy-paste Python lab

Analyst note: Data does not replace judgement — it sharpens it. This module teaches you to find open data, clean it lightly, chart trends, and recognise simple fraud signals. Everything runs in the browser for free.

LESSON 1

Finding Free Financial & Economic Datasets

You do not need a paid Bloomberg terminal to practise. High-quality open data is public.

Analogy: A public library for numbers. Anyone can borrow; you must still read carefully and cite the source.

Trusted starting points:

  • World Bank Open Data (data.worldbank.org) — GDP, inflation, credit to private sector, financial inclusion indicators by country.
  • RBI Database on Indian Economy — bank credit, deposits, payment system statistics (search “DBIE RBI”).
  • Kaggle Datasets (kaggle.com/datasets) — credit card fraud samples, loan data, stock CSVs (check licence; many are CC0 / open).
  • NSE / Yahoo Finance historical CSVs — for learning charts only (not trading advice).
  • data.gov.in — Indian government open datasets.

How to download (World Bank example): Search an indicator → select countries/years → Download → CSV. Open in Google Sheets via File → Import.

Everyday takeaway: Prefer official or well-documented sources; always note the date, units and definition of each column.
LESSON 2

Basic Cleaning and Trend Visualization

Raw files often have blank rows, mixed date formats or extra header lines. Cleaning is 80% of real analysis.

In Google Sheets (no code):

  1. Import CSV → remove empty rows.
  2. Fix dates: Data → Split text to columns or format cells as Date.
  3. Numbers stored as text: multiply the column by 1 or use VALUE().
  4. Chart: select year + value columns → Insert → Chart → Line chart for trends, Column for comparisons.
  5. Add a title, label axes, and write one sentence: “What changed and roughly when?”
Analogy: Cleaning is washing vegetables before cooking. Visualization is plating — so others can see the pattern without eating the raw spreadsheet.

Look for level shifts, seasonality (repeating ups and downs) and outliers (points far from the rest). Outliers are not always errors — sometimes they are the story (a crisis year, a policy change).

Everyday takeaway: Clean → chart → caption. If you cannot explain the chart in one plain sentence, simplify the chart.
LESSON 3

Using Data to Spot Suspicious Financial Transactions

Banks and FinTechs use rules + machine learning. As a beginner you can still learn the ideas behind simple fraud flags.

Analogy: A night guard who knows normal foot traffic. At 3 a.m., ten large packages leaving through the side door is unusual — worth a second look, not an automatic arrest.

Classic rule-style signals (illustrative):

  • Amount much larger than the customer’s usual spending
  • Sudden geography change (city A then city B within minutes)
  • Many small tests followed by a large transfer
  • New device + high-value payment in a short window
  • Round-number repeats that look automated

In a spreadsheet you might compute each customer’s average and standard deviation of transaction amounts, then flag rows where amount > average + 3× standard deviation. That is a teaching toy — real systems use richer features and careful human review to limit false positives.

Everyday takeaway: Fraud detection = “is this normal for this customer?” plus investigation. Data flags; people decide.
HANDS-ON LAB — GOOGLE COLAB (ONE-CLICK STYLE)

Run a Mini Visualization + Fraud-Flag Demo in Your Browser

Google Colab gives you a free Python notebook in the browser. No install.

Steps

  1. Open colab.research.google.com → New notebook.
  2. Click a cell → paste Block 1 below → press the Play button (or Shift+Enter).
  3. New cell → paste Block 2 → run. You should see a line chart and a small table of “flagged” rows.

Block 1 — create sample payment data and chart daily totals

# Block 1: sample payments + daily trend chart
# Works in Google Colab with one click (Shift+Enter)

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Reproducible "randomness" so everyone sees similar data
np.random.seed(42)

# 14 days of synthetic UPI-style payments for a few users
dates = pd.date_range("2026-08-01", periods=14, freq="D")
rows = []
for d in dates:
    # Normal small payments
    for user in ["U1", "U2", "U3", "U4"]:
        n = np.random.randint(2, 6)
        for _ in range(n):
            rows.append({
                "date": d,
                "user": user,
                "amount": round(float(np.random.lognormal(mean=5.5, sigma=0.4)), 2),
                "city": np.random.choice(["Bhubaneswar", "Cuttack", "Puri"])
            })

# Inject a few suspicious rows (large spikes)
rows.append({"date": dates[10], "user": "U2", "amount": 85000.0, "city": "Mumbai"})
rows.append({"date": dates[11], "user": "U2", "amount": 92000.0, "city": "Delhi"})
rows.append({"date": dates[5], "user": "U4", "amount": 1200.0, "city": "Bhubaneswar"})  # normal-ish

df = pd.DataFrame(rows)
print("Sample of transactions:")
print(df.head(8))
print("\nTotal rows:", len(df))

# Daily total amount (trend)
daily = df.groupby("date", as_index=False)["amount"].sum()

plt.figure(figsize=(9, 4))
plt.plot(daily["date"], daily["amount"], marker="o")
plt.title("Daily total payment volume (synthetic demo)")
plt.xlabel("Date")
plt.ylabel("Total amount")
plt.xticks(rotation=45)
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

Block 2 — simple per-user fraud flag (mean + 3× std)

# Block 2: flag amounts far above each user's normal range
# Teaching demo only — not a real bank fraud engine

# Average and std deviation of amount for each user
stats = df.groupby("user")["amount"].agg(["mean", "std"]).reset_index()
stats.columns = ["user", "user_mean", "user_std"]

# Join stats back to every transaction
merged = df.merge(stats, on="user", how="left")

# Flag if amount > mean + 3 * std (classic simple outlier rule)
merged["flag_outlier"] = merged["amount"] > (merged["user_mean"] + 3 * merged["user_std"])

flagged = merged[merged["flag_outlier"]].copy()
print("Flagged transactions (review candidates):")
print(flagged[["date", "user", "amount", "city", "user_mean"]].to_string(index=False))

print("\nInterpretation tip:")
print("Large jumps for U2 after normal history are worth a human check.")
print("Always measure false positives before blocking real customers.")

What you practised: Build a tiny dataset → plot a trend → apply a transparent rule to surface odd rows. Real systems add device data, velocity checks and ML — but the habit of “chart first, then question outliers” stays the same.

Module 5: Protecting Digital Money & Preventing Frauds

Security note: Most digital fraud succeeds by tricking people, not by breaking bank vaults. Learn the traps, then lock the doors on your own devices.

LESSON 1

Common FinTech Scams

Phishing: Fake SMS, email or WhatsApp that looks like your bank — “KYC expired, click here.” The link leads to a look-alike page that steals PINs or passwords.

SIM-swapping: Fraudsters convince a mobile operator to move your number to their SIM, then intercept OTPs.

Mule accounts: Someone asks you to receive and forward money “as a favour.” You become a link in a money-laundering chain and can face legal trouble.

UPI traps: Collect-request scams (you approve a request thinking you will receive money), QR codes that pay the wrong person, and “refund” calls that guide you to enter a PIN.

Rule: Banks never ask for full PIN/OTP on a call. If rushed or scared, pause and verify via the official app.
LESSON 2

How Systems Fight Back: MFA, Biometrics, Behavioural AI

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Something you know (PIN) + something you have (phone) + something you are (fingerprint/face).

Biometric locks: Reduce reliance on passwords you might reuse or share.

Behavioural AI: Models learn your normal login time, device and typing/swipe patterns. A sudden login from a new country with a large transfer can be challenged or blocked.

Analogy: A doorman who knows your face and usual hours — a stranger at 3 a.m. with your key still gets questioned.
LESSON 3

Data Privacy — Simple Global Picture

Laws such as the EU GDPR, India’s evolving data protection framework, and sector banking secrecy rules aim to ensure: consent, purpose limitation, security safeguards, and rights to access or correct data.

As a consumer: read permission prompts, revoke unused app access, and prefer official stores for banking apps.

DIY LAB

Digital Safety Checklist & Audit (Your Phone)

  1. Update OS and banking apps.
  2. Turn on app lock / biometric for every bank and UPI app.
  3. Disable SIM / SMS OTP forwarding features you do not use; set a strong SIM PIN with the operator.
  4. Review app permissions (SMS, phone, accessibility) — revoke odd ones.
  5. Check linked devices/sessions inside each banking app; log out unknowns.
  6. Use a unique password or passkey manager; never store full UPI PIN in notes.
  7. Practise: long-press a suspicious SMS link — do not open; delete.
  8. Write three personal rules on a note: e.g. “No PIN on phone calls,” “Verify collect requests,” “Official app only.”

Module 6: Artificial Intelligence & Chatbots in Finance

Curated by Dr. Debasis Pahi · Assistant Professor (OES-I), Model Degree College Malkangiri · Ph.D. (IIT Kharagpur)
LLMs · Algo trading concepts · Personalization · Prompt lab

AI PM note: AI in finance is a co-pilot. It speeds answers and patterns; humans still own accountability and edge cases.

LESSON 1

Conversational Banking & LLMs

Large language models power chatbots that answer balance queries, explain fees, and guide form-filling 24/7. Good systems stay within policy, escalate to humans, and never invent account numbers.

Analogy: A well-trained front-desk assistant who has the handbook open — fast for routine questions, transfers hard cases to a specialist.
LESSON 2

Algorithmic Trading (Plain View)

Programs scan prices and sometimes news headlines, then place orders in milliseconds. Useful for liquidity; risky if models amplify crashes. Retail learners should treat this as knowledge, not a get-rich system.

LESSON 3

Hyper-Personalization

Apps cluster your spending (food, travel, rent) and nudge savings goals or round-ups. Value appears when tips are timely and opt-out is easy; creepiness appears when data use is opaque.

Wealth note: Automation helps habits. It does not remove market risk or the need to understand fees.

LESSON 1

Robo-Advisors

Questionnaires capture goal and risk tolerance; algorithms allocate mostly low-cost funds and rebalance. Best for simple long-term goals; complex tax/estate needs still want human advice.

LESSON 2

Fractional Investing

Apps split expensive shares so ₹100 can buy a slice. Great for learning diversification; watch fees and liquidity.

LESSON 3

Micro-Savings / Round-Ups

Spend ₹49.50 → invest ₹0.50 automatically. Small amounts + time + compounding matter more than perfect stock picks for beginners.

Common Question Patterns & How to Solve Them

Most English comprehension tests follow predictable patterns. Master these and you will answer faster and more accurately.

1. Main Idea / Central Theme

Typical wording: “The primary purpose of the passage is…”, “The main idea of the passage is…”, “Which of the following best summarises…?”

How to solve: Look at the first and last paragraphs. Ignore specific examples. Choose the option that covers the whole passage, not just one part.

Trap: Options that are true but too narrow (only one paragraph).

2. Detail / Factual Questions

Typical wording: “According to the passage…”, “The author mentions that…”, “Which of the following is true…?”

How to solve: Scan for keywords from the question. Read the surrounding sentences carefully. The answer is almost always directly stated.

Trap: Options that use the same words but change the meaning slightly.

3. Inference Questions

Typical wording: “It can be inferred that…”, “The author implies…”, “Which of the following is most likely true…?”

How to solve: The answer is not stated directly but must be true based on the information given. Ask: “If the passage is true, which option must also be true?”

Trap: Extreme options or ideas that go far beyond the passage.

4. Vocabulary in Context

Typical wording: “The word ‘X’ in the passage most nearly means…”, “As used in the passage, ‘Y’ refers to…”

How to solve: Never rely on the dictionary meaning alone. Substitute each option into the sentence and see which fits the logic and tone.

Trap: The most common meaning of the word when the context requires a secondary meaning.

5. Tone / Attitude / Purpose

Typical wording: “The tone of the passage is…”, “The author’s attitude towards… is…”

How to solve: Look for positive, negative or neutral language. Words like “however”, “despite”, “unfortunately” signal contrast or caution.

Common tones: Balanced, optimistic, critical, cautionary, informative, persuasive.

6. Except / Not Questions

Typical wording: “All of the following are mentioned EXCEPT…”, “Which of the following is NOT true according to the passage?”

How to solve: Check each option against the passage. Three will be supported; the one that is missing or contradicted is the answer.

Proven Reading Strategies

1. Read the questions first (30 seconds)

Quickly glance at the questions before reading the passage. This tells your brain what information to look for and improves focus.

2. Skim for structure, then read carefully

  • First 10–15 seconds: identify topic and structure (problem–solution, cause–effect, comparison, chronological).
  • Then read carefully, underlining or noting key transition words: however, therefore, in contrast, for example, in conclusion.

3. Active reading checklist

  • What is the author’s main point?
  • What evidence or examples support it?
  • Is the tone positive, negative or balanced?
  • Are there any contrasts or unexpected turns?

4. Elimination technique

Cross out options that are:

  • Too extreme (always, never, only, completely, all)
  • Outside the scope of the passage
  • True in general life but not supported by the text
  • Using passage words but twisting the meaning

5. Time management rule of thumb

For a typical set of 6 questions on one passage: spend about 3–4 minutes reading + 5–6 minutes answering. Do not spend more than 90 seconds on any single question. Mark and move on if stuck.

6. Daily practice habit (recommended)

  • Read one quality article daily (Mint, The Hindu Business Line, BBC Business, or any FinTech news).
  • After reading, write in one sentence: “The main idea is…”
  • Pick 5 new words and note their meaning in context.
  • Complete at least one practice set from this app every day until the exam.

Quick Reference – Transition Words

Contrast: however, although, despite, whereas, on the other hand, nevertheless

Cause–Effect: therefore, consequently, as a result, thus, hence, because

Addition: moreover, furthermore, in addition, also, besides

Example: for instance, for example, such as, namely

Conclusion: in conclusion, overall, in summary, ultimately

Exam-Day Tips for the Virtual English Comprehension Test

Before the test

  • Ensure stable internet, charged device and a quiet environment.
  • Keep a rough sheet or digital notepad ready for notes (if allowed).
  • Do a short warm-up: read one article and answer 2–3 questions from this app.
  • Sleep well the night before – comprehension drops sharply when you are tired.

During the test

  • Read questions first, then the passage.
  • Answer the easier detail questions first; leave inference questions for later if needed.
  • For vocabulary questions, always go back to the exact sentence.
  • Never leave a question blank if there is no negative marking (most virtual tests do not penalise wrong answers).
  • Watch the clock but do not panic – accuracy is more important than speed.

Common traps to avoid

  • Options that are factually correct in the real world but not stated or implied in the passage.
  • Extreme language (always / never / only / completely).
  • Answers that repeat words from the passage but change the logical relationship.
  • Choosing an option just because it “sounds smart”.
