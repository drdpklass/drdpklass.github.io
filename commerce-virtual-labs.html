Aim

See why two companies can depreciate the identical asset — and report completely different profits.

You'll compare the Written Down Value (WDV) method against the Straight Line Method (SLM) on the same asset, and watch how each one shapes the book value and the expense hitting the P&L, year by year.

Theory

Straight Line spreads the depreciable amount evenly across the asset's useful life. WDV instead charges a fixed percentage of whatever book value remains — so the expense is largest in year one and shrinks every year after.

SLM: (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Life   |   WDV: Opening Book Value × Rate%

Aim

Run the same stock movements through FIFO and Weighted Average — and watch profit change without a single extra rupee of sales.

You'll set an opening stock, one purchase batch, and a quantity sold. The units and the sale are identical under both methods — only the costing assumption differs.

Theory

FIFO assumes the oldest stock leaves the warehouse first, so the closing balance is always priced at the newest cost. Weighted Average blends every rupee spent into one rate the moment new stock arrives, so a single price carries through the sale and the closing stock alike.

Weighted Avg Rate = Total cost of stock available ÷ Total units available

Aim

Feel why bond prices fall when interest rates rise — before you ever memorise the rule.

Move the market yield and watch the bond's price react in real time. Then look at the curve: it isn't a straight line, and that curvature has a name you'll meet later — convexity.

Theory

A bond's price is the present value of every future coupon plus the present value of the face value returned at maturity, all discounted at the market's required yield. When the yield you can get elsewhere rises, this bond's fixed coupons become less attractive — so its price must fall to compensate.

Price = Σ Coupon ÷ (1+y)ᵗ  +  Face Value ÷ (1+y)ⁿ

Aim

Split the gap between actual and standard material cost into what you paid for it, and how much of it you used.

Set a standard price and quantity, then move the actual price paid and the actual quantity consumed. Watch the total material cost variance separate cleanly into a price variance and a usage variance.

Theory

The price variance isolates what the purchasing team controls — did we pay more or less per kg than standard, on the quantity actually bought? The usage variance isolates what the production team controls — did the process consume more or less material than the standard allows for the output actually made.

Price Var = (Std Price − Actual Price) × Actual Qty   |   Usage Var = (Std Qty Allowed − Actual Qty) × Std Price

Aim

See how ESOPs and convertible instruments dilute earnings per share — even while net profit is growing.

Set a net profit and an existing share count, then add potential shares from options or convertibles. Watch diluted EPS pull away from basic EPS as the denominator grows.

Theory

Basic EPS divides profit by the shares that actually exist today. Diluted EPS asks a harder question: if every convertible instrument and vested option were exercised right now, how much would each share be worth? Because that adds shares without adding profit, diluted EPS can never exceed basic EPS.

Basic EPS = Net Profit ÷ Existing Shares   |   Diluted EPS = Net Profit ÷ (Existing Shares + Potential Shares)

Aim

Find the exact point where a business stops losing money and starts making it.

Set a price, a variable cost, and a fixed cost base — then watch the break-even point move as you change any one of them. This is the single most useful number in a founder's or manager's head.

Theory

Every unit sold contributes its selling price, minus its variable cost, toward covering the fixed costs. Once enough units have been sold to cover fixed costs entirely, every further unit is pure profit.

Break-even units = Fixed Costs ÷ (Price − Variable Cost)
